RATIONAL THINKING · EPISODE 02

## 1. A room I've never seen

Imagine a room you've never visited.

You don't know how large it is. You don't know how many people are inside. And you certainly don't know how many chairs it contains.

But I tell you just two things.

Every table has exactly three chairs around it.

And there are two additional chairs near the entrance.

That's all.

<ReasoningFigure kind="room" />

Now, here's a strange question.

Could the room contain fourteen chairs?

Maybe.

But what about fifteen?

I claim that fifteen chairs are impossible.

And that's a surprisingly strong claim, isn't it?

After all, we haven't counted the chairs. We haven't even seen the room.

So how could we possibly know that?

Perhaps we could start listing arrangements and checking which numbers appear.

But there's a better way.

We can discover something about the total number of chairs without knowing the total itself.

And to understand how, we need to explore an idea that sits at the heart of algebra.

The idea that a letter can sometimes tell us more than a number.

## 2. What changes, and what doesn't?

Let's examine a few possible rooms.

Suppose there's only one table.

We need three chairs around it, and the two chairs near the entrance.

That's five chairs.

Now add another table.

We need three more chairs. So our total becomes eight.

Add a third table, and we get eleven.

A fourth, and we get fourteen.

Look carefully at these arrangements.

The total number of chairs keeps changing. But not everything changes.

Each new table contributes exactly the same thing: three chairs.

And the two chairs near the entrance never change at all.

So the room contains two different kinds of quantities.

One that stays fixed.

And another that depends on how many tables we have.

We could continue calculating indefinitely. But no matter how many examples we check, the same structure keeps appearing.

Groups of three.

And two additional chairs.

So rather than listing every possible room, what if we described the structure that all these rooms share?

<ReasoningFigure kind="table-pattern" />

## 3. Replacing a number with an idea

We know that every table requires three chairs.

But we still don't know how many tables the room contains.

So let's do something simple.

Instead of writing a particular number of tables, let's give that quantity a name.

We could call it almost anything. But mathematicians often use letters.

Let's choose $n$.

From now on, $n$ represents the number of tables.

Not the number of chairs. Not the number of people.

The number of tables.

And because every table needs three chairs, the chairs around the tables must total three times $n$.

We can write that as three multiplied by $n$.

Or simply, three $n$.

Now remember the two chairs near the entrance.

Those must be added to the total.

And so we arrive at this:

$$ \boxed{3n+2} $$

Three times the number of tables, plus two.

This is called an algebraic expression.

It's a combination of mathematical symbols that represents a quantity.

And notice what we've accomplished.

We haven't calculated the exact number of chairs.

We've described the relationship that determines that number.

Choose four tables, and the expression gives fourteen chairs.

Choose six, and it gives twenty.

The particular answer changes.

But the rule doesn't.

## 4. An answer we can rule out without counting

Now we can return to our original question.

Why did I claim that the room cannot contain fifteen chairs?

Let's think about what fifteen chairs would mean.

We know that two chairs are always near the entrance.

If the total were fifteen, removing those two would leave thirteen chairs around the tables.

But every table must have exactly three chairs.

Can thirteen chairs be divided into complete groups of three?

No.

Four groups of three would use twelve chairs, leaving one chair without a complete group.

And that's not permitted by our arrangement.

So fifteen cannot be the total.

We can express this same question mathematically.

If three $n$ plus two were equal to fifteen, then three $n$ would have to equal thirteen.

But no whole number of tables multiplied by three gives thirteen.

So there is no permitted value of $n$ that works.

<ReasoningFigure kind="impossible-chairs" />

Notice what makes this reasoning different from checking examples.

We didn't examine every possible room.

We used the structure of the arrangement to rule out an entire possibility.

And that's a powerful form of knowledge.

We can establish something about an answer without ever knowing the answer itself.

## 5. When two extra chairs become two extra tables

Now let's make a tiny change to our room.

Instead of having two extra chairs near the entrance, imagine we have two extra tables.

And each of those tables also needs three chairs.

Does our original expression still describe the room?

Think about what changed.

Previously, we counted three chairs for each of our $n$ tables, and then added two individual chairs.

Now we're adding two complete tables.

So the total number of tables is $n$ plus two.

And since every one of those tables needs three chairs, we multiply that entire quantity by three.

We write:

$$ 3(n+2) $$

The curved marks are called parentheses.

They show that $n$ plus two forms a group, and that the three multiplies everything inside it.

Compare that with our original expression.

$$ 3n+2 \qquad\text{and}\qquad 3(n+2) $$

If $n$ is two, the first arrangement has eight chairs.

But the second has twelve.

Why four more?

Because two extra tables require six chairs, whereas our original arrangement added only two.

In fact, the second expression is the same as three $n$ plus six.

Those parentheses have changed which quantities the multiplication applies to.

They're not a decorative feature of mathematical writing.

They're part of what the expression means.

$$ 3(n+2)=3n+6 $$

## 6. The three different things a letter can mean

There's another subtlety.

We've been using the same letter, $n$, throughout our investigation.

But we've actually been asking different kinds of questions about it.

When we imagined one table, then two, then three, $n$ was allowed to change.

In that situation, we call $n$ a variable.

It represents a quantity that can take different values.

But when we asked whether the room could contain fifteen chairs, we were looking for a particular value of $n$ that would make an equation true.

There, $n$ was an unknown.

And when we reasoned that every permitted arrangement must contain complete groups of three plus two extra chairs, $n$ had a third role.

We weren't looking for a particular value at all.

We were reasoning about an arbitrary number of tables.

Any allowed number.

These aren't three different kinds of letters.

They're three different ways to use a mathematical symbol.

And the important question is never simply, "What does $n$ equal?"

Sometimes there isn't supposed to be one particular value.

Sometimes we're trying to understand what must remain true as that value changes.

## 7. When a correct calculation gives a meaningless answer

But if $n$ can change, how far can it change?

What happens if we choose two and a half?

We can certainly calculate three times two and a half, plus two.

That gives nine and a half.

There's nothing wrong with the arithmetic.

But does our room really contain two and a half tables, each with three chairs?

Not under the rules we've established.

Our tables are complete objects. We count them using whole numbers.

Zero tables, one table, two tables, and so on.

Mathematicians call the collection of permitted values the domain.

For complete tables, the permitted values are the nonnegative integers:

$$ n\in\mathbb{N}_0=\{0,1,2,3,\ldots\} $$

And the domain depends on what the letter represents.

If we're counting complete tables, fractional values don't belong to our domain.

But if we're measuring time, one and a half hours might be perfectly sensible.

And here's the deeper lesson.

A mathematical expression doesn't tell us everything about its own interpretation.

We have to know what its symbols refer to.

The same calculation can be mathematically valid, yet fail to describe the situation we intended.

## 8. Mathematics is a language, not just a calculation

Let's try a different situation.

Imagine you're buying tickets to an event.

Each ticket costs five euros, and there's a single booking fee of one euro for the entire purchase.

You don't yet know how many tickets you'll buy.

How could we describe the total price?

Let's call the number of tickets $n$. For a purchase, $n$ is a positive whole number.

Each ticket contributes five euros. So $n$ tickets cost five times $n$.

And then we add the one-euro booking fee.

$$ 5n+1 $$

Now imagine that I show you only that expression, without telling you the story.

Could you discover the original situation?

Not necessarily.

It might describe ticket prices, or the number of objects in a collection, or something completely different.

The symbols preserve the mathematical relationship, but not every detail of the story.

And even small differences in ordinary language can change that relationship.

Suppose the booking fee is one euro per ticket, instead of one euro for the whole purchase.

Then every ticket costs six euros in total.

And the expression becomes six $n$.

The words have changed slightly.

But the mathematical structure has changed.

This is why translating between pictures, language, and symbols requires understanding what each quantity actually means.

Not merely recognizing familiar numbers and operations.

## 9. When mathematical rules meet the real world

Now consider a scientific example.

Imagine a chamber whose temperature begins at eighteen degrees Celsius.

A heater raises its temperature by two degrees every hour.

At least, that's the rule we're assuming.

How could we express its temperature after an arbitrary amount of time?

Let's call the number of hours $t$.

We begin at eighteen degrees.

And add two degrees for each hour.

So the temperature is described by:

$$ 18+2t $$

After one hour, our model predicts twenty degrees.

After one and a half hours, it predicts twenty-one.

But here's a question.

What happens if we put one thousand in place of $t$?

The expression gives two thousand and eighteen degrees Celsius.

Does that mean the chamber will actually reach that temperature?

Not necessarily.

Our calculation assumes the temperature keeps rising at exactly the same rate.

A real heating system might shut off. Heat losses might increase. Its components might fail.

The mathematical conclusion follows from the assumed rule.

But whether that rule continues to describe reality must be established independently.

That's an important distinction.

Mathematics tells us what follows from our assumptions.

Science also asks whether those assumptions describe the world accurately.

## 10. What can we know without knowing the number?

Before we finish, here's one last problem.

A warehouse packages candles in boxes of four.

There are also three loose candles that haven't been packed.

You don't know how many boxes the warehouse contains.

But someone claims there are exactly twenty-four candles.

Could that be true?

Think about the structure.

<Exercise>

Every complete box contributes four candles. And there are always three loose ones.

So if we call the number of boxes $n$, the total must be four $n$ plus three.

Now remove the three loose candles from the claimed total of twenty-four.

That leaves twenty-one.

But twenty-one cannot be divided into complete groups of four.

So the claim is impossible, provided the packing rule is correct.

<ReasoningFigure kind="candles" />

</Exercise>

And notice how little information we needed.

We didn't have to count the boxes.

We didn't have to see the warehouse.

We only needed to understand the relationship.

That's what a letter has allowed us to express.

Not a particular answer, but the structure shared by every permitted answer.

The numbers may change. The situation may change. Even the objects themselves may change.

But if the same mathematical relationship remains, we can reason about all those possibilities together.

Of course, the conclusions we draw still depend on what our symbols mean and which assumptions we're making.

A letter doesn't remove the need to think carefully.

It gives that thinking a more precise language.

And once we've described a relationship that seems to hold for every permitted case, another question naturally follows.

How can we establish that it really holds for all of them?

Not just the examples we've seen.

Every single one.

The room and the warehouse share an idea: complete equal-sized groups plus a fixed number of extras.

$$ 3n+2\qquad 4n+3 $$
