## 1. The mystery of two correct answers

Imagine I show you a simple geometric shape.

<ReasoningFigure kind="validity-shape" />

Now suppose two people examine this shape and reach exactly the same conclusion:

"Shape S is a square."

The first person explains:

Person A

"Shape S has four equal sides and four right angles. Any quadrilateral with these properties is a square. Therefore, Shape S is a square."

The second person says:

Person B

"Every square is a rectangle. Shape S is a rectangle. Therefore, Shape S is a square."

Both people have reached the same conclusion.

And that conclusion is true.

But there's a problem.

One argument establishes the conclusion. The other doesn't.

Why?

After all, both people have said something correct. Every premise they've used is true, and both conclusions are true.

So what exactly is different?

<Interactive kind="validity" preset="opening" id="validity-opening" />

This is a surprisingly important distinction.

Because when someone tells us something, we often evaluate their reasoning by asking one question:

Were they right?

But being right and having a good reason for being right are two different things.

You can arrive at the truth through understanding. You can arrive at it by guessing. And sometimes you can arrive at it through reasoning that doesn't actually justify your conclusion.

To understand the difference, we're going to take these two arguments apart.

And we'll discover that the most important question in deductive logic isn't simply whether something is true.

It's whether the conclusion had to be true, given the premises.

## 2. An argument is a bridge between statements

Let's start with Person B's argument.

"Every square is a rectangle. Shape S is a rectangle. Therefore, Shape S is a square."

We can separate it into three statements.

<ReasoningFigure kind="validity-argument" />

In logic, we call the statements offered as reasons premises.

And the statement we're trying to establish is the conclusion.

So an argument is a collection of statements in which the premises are presented as support for the conclusion.

But notice that the diagram contains two different things.

There are the statements themselves.

And then there are the arrows connecting them.

Those arrows represent the claim that the premises justify the conclusion.

And that's where a logical mistake can hide.

Because even if each individual statement is true, the connection between them might not be justified.

Let's make that visible.

## 3. One drawing reveals the hidden mistake

Let's forget the arguments for a moment and think about what it means for something to be a square.

A square has four equal sides and four right angles.

A rectangle must have four right angles, but its sides don't all need to be equal.

So every square is a rectangle.

But does that mean every rectangle is a square?

We can visualize the relationship using two regions.

The large blue region contains all rectangles.

The smaller golden region contains all squares.

</Note>

<ReasoningFigure kind="validity-regions" />

Everything inside the gold region must also belong to the blue region.

Now focus on Shape S.

It belongs to the gold region, so it necessarily belongs to the blue region too.

If you know something is a square, you know it must be a rectangle.

The implication goes in this direction:

<ReasoningFigure kind="validity-forward" />

But now suppose we try the arrow in the opposite direction.

<ReasoningFigure kind="validity-reverse" />

There is only one way to find out.

We must look for a rectangle that isn't a square.

And here's where I'd like you to make a prediction.

What happens to Person B's reasoning if we replace Shape S with a rectangle whose sides aren't all equal?

<Interactive kind="validity" preset="countermodel" id="validity-countermodel" />

This is the key moment.

We've changed the shape, but we haven't changed the logical form of the argument.

Every square is still a rectangle.

Our new shape R is still a rectangle.

Yet the conclusion, that R is a square, is false.

We have discovered a possibility that the reasoning failed to exclude.

And that is all we need to demonstrate the logical flaw.

This technique has a name: constructing a countermodel.

It's a situation in which the premises are true and the conclusion is false.

But notice something subtle.

We haven't proved that the original Shape S wasn't a square.

It was a square. It still is.

Instead, we've proved that Person B's reasoning is unreliable as a deductive argument.

And now we can finally define validity precisely.

## 4. What does it really mean for an argument to be valid?

A deductive argument is valid when there is no possible situation in which all its premises are true and its conclusion is false.

That is the entire idea.

<ReasoningFigure kind="validity-test" />

Let's look at the two directions one final time.

<ReasoningFigure kind="validity-directions" />

Mathematicians express these patterns using implication.

Let $Q(x)$ mean that object $x$ is a square, and $R(x)$ mean that it is a rectangle.

We know:

$$ \forall x\,(Q(x)\Rightarrow R(x)). $$

This means every square is a rectangle.

If we also know $Q(S)$, we can conclude $R(S)$.

But knowing $R(S)$ doesn't let us reverse the implication.

$$ Q\Rightarrow R \quad\not\Rightarrow\quad R\Rightarrow Q. $$

The mistake of inferring the condition from its consequence is a classic fallacy known as affirming the consequent.

And it appears far beyond geometry.

"If it rains, the pavement becomes wet. The pavement is wet. Therefore, it rained."

Maybe it did.

But perhaps someone used a sprinkler.

Once again, a true observation doesn't guarantee the proposed explanation.

Validity is not about whether the conclusion happens to be true. It is about whether the premises leave any possibility for the conclusion to be false.

## 5. Four possibilities you probably didn't expect

We now know why Person B's reasoning fails.

But there's an even stranger consequence.

We've seen an invalid argument with a true conclusion.

Could the opposite also happen? Could a logically valid argument have a false conclusion?

Surprisingly, yes.

To understand why, we're going to arrange four arguments in a grid.

Remember our two shapes: S is a square, and R is a rectangle that isn't a square.

<ReasoningFigure kind="validity-grid" />

Look at the top-right example.

"All rectangles are squares. R is a rectangle. Therefore, R is a square."

The conclusion is false.

But the structure is valid.

Because if every rectangle really were a square, then anything known to be a rectangle would necessarily be a square.

The logical connection works. The starting information doesn't.

Now look at the bottom-left example.

The conclusion is true, because S really is a square.

But we've already demonstrated that the reasoning isn't valid.

These are two independent questions.

One concerns the truth of the conclusion.

The other concerns whether the premises guarantee that conclusion.

And this gives us a more complete picture:

Valid reasoning doesn't automatically give us true conclusions. True conclusions don't automatically indicate valid reasoning.

So what happens when we combine the two things we actually need?

## 6. Soundness: when the reasoning and the starting information are correct

Imagine that every argument must pass through two gates.

The first gate examines the reasoning.

The second gate examines the premises.

<ReasoningFigure kind="validity-gates" />

An argument that passes both gates is called sound.

A sound deductive argument is valid, and all its premises are actually true.

$$ \boxed{\text{Sound}=\text{Valid}+\text{True premises}} $$

Why does this guarantee the conclusion?

Because validity tells us that true premises cannot lead to a false conclusion.

And soundness tells us the premises really are true.

Now consider a slightly different situation.

Imagine we discover a sealed box of geometric shapes.

A researcher tells us:

"Every object inside this box is a square. Object K is inside the box. Therefore, K is a square."

Does the conclusion follow from the premises?

Yes. Perfectly.

But we haven't opened the box, and the researcher hasn't provided evidence that every object is a square.

So is the argument sound?

We can't establish that yet.

The first premise might be true. It might be false. We simply don't know.

This distinction is important.

An unsupported premise is not automatically a false premise.

Sometimes our reasoning is correct, but we lack sufficient information to establish that its premises are true.

And that is where logic connects to science.

Logic examines what follows from our assumptions.

Empirical investigation helps us determine whether those assumptions accurately describe the world.

Neither replaces the other.

And there's one more distinction worth carrying forward.

Today we've been analyzing deductive arguments, which aim to guarantee their conclusions.

But much of scientific and everyday reasoning is inductive or probabilistic. Evidence can make a conclusion more likely without making it logically inevitable.

A scientific explanation doesn't become worthless simply because the evidence fails to deductively guarantee it.

That will become important when we study probability, uncertainty, and evidence.

## 8. The final lesson: truth isn't the same as justification

Let's return to where we began.

Two people looked at the same square.

Two people said something true.

But only one gave an argument that established its conclusion.

And we now understand why.

Because an argument has two parts that we must never confuse: the statements themselves and the logical relationship between them.

A conclusion can be true even when the argument supporting it fails.

An argument can be valid even when the information it begins with is false.

And when valid reasoning starts from true premises, we have something stronger: a sound deductive argument.

Being correct and being justified are not the same achievement.

<ReasoningFigure kind="validity-distinction" />

This changes the question we should ask when someone presents an argument.

Instead of immediately asking whether we agree with its conclusion, we can ask something more fundamental:

Even if everything you started with were true, would your conclusion necessarily follow?

And only after answering that question do we investigate whether the starting information is actually true.

Because the aim of rational thinking is not merely to arrive at correct answers.

It is to understand why those answers deserve to be believed.

## Learning checkpoint

By the end of this episode, you should be able to explain:

- Premises and conclusion: what information an argument starts with and what it attempts to establish.
- Validity: why true premises must guarantee the conclusion in a valid deductive argument.
- Countermodels: how one possible case with true premises and a false conclusion demonstrates invalidity.
- Soundness: why both validity and true premises are necessary for a sound deductive argument.
- The central distinction: why identifying a bad argument does not, by itself, disprove its conclusion.
