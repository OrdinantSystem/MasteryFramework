RATIONAL THINKING · EPISODE 03

## 1. The equation that gained an answer

In this lesson, all numbers are real unless a further restriction is stated.

Imagine I tell you that I've chosen a number.

I'll call it $x$.

And suppose I give you one piece of information about that number:

$$
x=2
$$

There's not much mystery here. I've told you exactly what the number is.

But now, let's do something perfectly ordinary with this equation.

Let's square both sides.

If $x$ equals two, then $x$ squared must equal four.

$$
x^2=4
$$

So far, everything seems reasonable.

We've started with something true, performed the same operation on both sides, and arrived at something else that's true.

But look more closely at the new equation.

What values of $x$ could make it true?

Two certainly works.

But negative two also works, because multiplying negative two by itself gives positive four.

So somehow, we've taken an equation with exactly one possible solution and transformed it into an equation with two.

<ReasoningFigure kind="squared-solutions" />

And here's the strange part.

We haven't made a calculation mistake.

Two squared really is four.

The new equation really is true.

Yet we've gained a possibility that wasn't permitted by the original statement.

Where did that extra answer come from?

Did squaring somehow change the meaning of the equation?

Or is there something about the equations themselves that we're not paying attention to?

To understand what's happening, we need to investigate a symbol so familiar that we rarely stop to think about it.

The equals sign.

## 2. What equality actually tells us

Think back to the first arithmetic problems you learned.

You probably saw equations like this:

$$
3+4=7
$$

Three plus four equals seven.

And because most elementary calculations are written this way, it's easy to begin thinking that the equals sign means something like "now calculate the answer."

You start with a calculation on the left, and the result appears on the right.

But what happens if we reverse the equation?

$$
7=3+4
$$

Is it still true?

Of course.

Or suppose we write:

$$
3+4=2+5
$$

Now both sides contain calculations.

Neither side is more of an answer than the other.

And yet, the equation is perfectly valid.

To understand why, imagine seven stones lying on a table.

You could arrange them into a group of three and a group of four.

Or you could rearrange those same stones into a group of two and a group of five.

<Interactive kind="equations" preset="stones" id="seven-stones" />

Notice what changes and what stays the same.

The arrangement changes.

The numbers in each group change.

Even the expressions we use to describe the arrangement change.

But the total quantity remains exactly the same.

And that's what the equals sign is telling us.

It isn't an instruction to calculate.

It's a claim that two expressions represent the same value.

Once we understand equality this way, several important properties follow naturally.

If three plus four equals seven, then seven must equal three plus four.

It doesn't matter which expression we write first. This is called symmetry.

And if three plus four equals seven, while seven also equals two plus five, then three plus four must equal two plus five.

This is called transitivity.

But perhaps the most useful consequence is that we can substitute one expression for another whenever we know they're equal.

Since $3+4=7$, we can replace the expression $3+4$ with $7$, even when it's part of a larger calculation.

For example:

$$
2(3+4)=2(7)=14
$$

What makes all these operations reasonable isn't that we've memorized a rule about symbols.

It's that equal expressions represent the same value.

And this gives us an interesting possibility.

If equality tells us that two expressions represent the same quantity, perhaps we can use that relationship to discover a quantity we don't yet know.

## 3. What can we change without changing the truth?

Imagine a perfectly balanced scale.

On one side, there's a sealed box containing an unknown weight, together with three kilograms.

On the other side, there are eight kilograms.

We don't know how much the box weighs.

But because the scale is balanced, we know something about the relationship between the two sides.

The box, together with three kilograms, must weigh exactly eight kilograms.

Let's call the weight of the box $x$. Here $x$ is its numerical mass in kilograms.

Then we can describe the situation with an equation:

$$
x+3=8
$$

Now suppose we want to discover the weight of the box.

We could try guessing.

But instead, let's use what we know about equality.

If we remove three kilograms from the left side, the balance would be disturbed.

Unless we also remove three kilograms from the right.

Then both sides have lost the same amount of weight.

And the relationship between them is preserved.

<Interactive kind="equations" preset="balance" id="equal-balance" />

And now, after removing those three kilograms from each side, the box is alone on the left, while five kilograms remain on the right.

So the box must weigh five kilograms.

Algebra expresses the very same reasoning.

$$
\begin{aligned} x+3&=8\\ x+3-3&=8-3\\ x&=5 \end{aligned}
$$

But here's something important.

We can also reverse the entire process.

If the box weighs five kilograms, then adding three kilograms to it gives eight kilograms.

So starting with $x=5$, we can recover our original equation.

We're not merely creating a new true statement.

We're describing exactly the same situation in a different way.

Any value of $x$ that makes the first equation true also makes the second true.

And any value that makes the second true also makes the first true.

The two equations permit exactly the same possibilities.

This is the kind of transformation we want when solving equations.

We want to simplify the mathematical relationship without accidentally changing which values satisfy it.

But how can we actually tell whether we've succeeded?

We need a way to examine not only the expressions in an equation, but the values that make the entire statement true.

## 4. Every equation tells us which possibilities remain

Consider this equation:

$$
x+3=3+x
$$

If $x$ is two, both sides equal five.

If $x$ is ten, both sides equal thirteen.

And if $x$ is negative three, both sides equal zero.

In fact, this equation works for every real number.

That's because changing the order in which we add two numbers doesn't change their sum.

An equation that's true for every value in its stated domain is called an identity.

Now compare it with our balance equation:

$$
x+3=8
$$

That equation isn't true for every number.

It works only when $x$ equals five.

<Interactive kind="equations" preset="identity" id="identity-and-solution" />

And this gives us a useful way to think about an equation.

For every number we're allowed to consider, the equation either accepts that value or rejects it.

When we solve an equation, we're trying to discover the complete collection of values it accepts.

So an important question emerges.

When we transform one equation into another, have we preserved that collection?

If yes, we haven't lost or gained any possible solutions.

But if the collection changes, then something about the information the equation gives us has changed as well.

And now we're ready to return to our original mystery.

## 5. How a true statement can lose information

We started by knowing exactly what $x$ was.

$$
x=2
$$

But after squaring, we obtained:

$$
x^2=4
$$

And somehow, we gained an extra possibility.

To see why, let's forget equations for a moment.

Imagine a machine that squares whatever number we give it.

Put two into the machine, and four comes out.

Put negative two into the machine, and four comes out again.

Two different inputs have produced the same output.

Now imagine that you can only see the output.

You see the number four.

Could you tell me which number entered the machine?

Not necessarily.

It might have been two, or it might have been negative two.

The process of squaring has made those two inputs indistinguishable.

In other words, the operation has lost information about the original input.

We can see this directly if we draw a graph of the squaring operation.

<Interactive kind="equations" preset="square" id="square-information" />

Notice how the graph is symmetrical.

Whenever we choose a number on one side of zero, there's another number on the opposite side that produces the same squared value.

The operation preserves the fact that equal numbers have equal squares.

But it doesn't allow us to recover every original number uniquely.

And that's precisely what happened to our equation.

When $x=2$, the equation $x^2=4$ must also be true.

But knowing only that $x^2=4$ doesn't tell us whether $x$ was two or negative two.

Let's compare this with an everyday example.

If I tell you that someone is exactly twenty years old, then you know that they're older than ten.

But if I only tell you that they're older than ten, you can't conclude that they're exactly twenty.

The second statement follows from the first, but it gives you less information.

Our equations work in a similar way.

The first equation allows only one value.

The second allows two.

This is where we need a new concept.

When the truth of one statement guarantees the truth of another, we call that an implication.

We write:

$$
x=2\quad\Longrightarrow\quad x^2=4
$$

The arrow tells us that the first statement guarantees the second.

But it doesn't promise that we can reverse the reasoning.

To do that, we would need to know something more.

For example, suppose I also tell you that $x$ cannot be negative.

Now, if $x^2=4$, only one possibility remains.

Two.

The lost information has been restored through an additional condition.

<Interactive kind="equations" preset="restriction" id="recover-sign" />

And now we can understand the difference between two mathematical relationships.

An implication tells us that one statement guarantees another.

An equivalence tells us that each statement guarantees the other.

We write a double arrow when both directions work.

For example:

$$
x+3=8\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad x=5
$$

Subtracting three takes us from the first equation to the second.

Adding three takes us back.

Both equations admit exactly the same values.

But squaring $x=2$ gives us only a one-way implication unless we preserve an additional condition that rules out negative two.

This is why performing the same operation on both sides isn't enough to guarantee that we've preserved all the original information.

The operation may produce a true statement while allowing more possibilities than before.

And once we understand that, we can recognize another kind of mistake—one where we lose a genuine solution instead of gaining an extra one.

## 6. The answer that disappeared

Consider this equation:

$$
x(x-2)=0
$$

We want to discover which values of $x$ make it true.

Now imagine someone tries to solve it by dividing both sides by $x$.

The $x$ on the left cancels, and they're left with:

$$
x-2=0
$$

So they conclude that $x=2$.

And if we substitute two into the original equation, it works.

Two multiplied by zero is zero.

So the answer appears correct.

But let's try something else.

What happens when $x$ equals zero?

The original expression becomes zero multiplied by negative two.

And that also equals zero.

So zero is another perfectly valid solution.

Where did it disappear?

The problem lies in the step where we divided by $x$.

Because dividing by $x$ is only allowed when $x$ isn't zero.

We silently introduced a restriction that excluded one of the original solutions.

<Interactive kind="equations" preset="division" id="division-restriction" />

So how should we solve this equation correctly?

Instead of immediately dividing, let's examine what the original relationship tells us.

We have two factors being multiplied together, and their product is zero.

A product of two real numbers is zero precisely when at least one of its factors is zero.

So either the first factor is zero, or the second factor is zero.

$$
x=0\quad\text{or}\quad x-2=0
$$

And that gives us:

$$
\boxed{x=0\quad\text{or}\quad x=2}
$$

Both values satisfy the original equation.

Now compare this mistake with the one we made by squaring.

When we squared $x=2$, we gained a solution.

When we divided by $x$, we lost a solution.

But both mistakes teach us the same general lesson.

We cannot judge an algebraic transformation merely by how simple its result looks.

We need to understand what happens to the possible solutions.

And whenever an operation requires a condition, that condition is part of the reasoning.

This gives us a much more reliable way to solve equations.

Rather than asking only whether we've performed an operation correctly, we ask whether the new equation describes exactly the same possibilities as the original.

And we can test whether we've really understood that idea by applying it to a completely different situation.

## 7. A final challenge: the answer that isn't complete

Imagine a number line.

Place a point at three.

Now I want you to find every number exactly two units away from that point.

If we move two units to the right, we arrive at five.

But if we move two units to the left, we arrive at one.

So there are two answers.

We can describe this mathematically using absolute value.

$$
|x-3|=2
$$

The vertical bars represent absolute value. For real numbers, the absolute value of $x-3$ is the distance between $x$ and three.

Now imagine someone removes those bars, leaving:

$$
x-3=2
$$

They solve the equation and obtain five.

Is five a valid solution?

Absolutely.

But is it the complete answer?

<Interactive kind="equations" preset="distance" id="two-directions" />

The problem is that the original equation describes a distance.

And distance doesn't specify a direction.

When we replaced the absolute-value equation with $x-3=2$, we considered only the point to the right of three.

To preserve every possibility, we also need to consider:

$$
x-3=-2
$$

So our complete solution is:

$$
\boxed{x=1\quad\text{or}\quad x=5}
$$

Notice what we've learned to recognize.

The answer five wasn't false.

The calculation that produced it was valid for one of the possibilities.

But finding a correct answer isn't necessarily the same as finding every correct answer.

And that's an important distinction, not just in algebra, but in reasoning generally.

A conclusion may follow correctly from what we know without capturing everything that the original information tells us.

## 8. What the equals sign promises

So let's return to our original question.

What does the equals sign actually promise?

It tells us that two expressions represent the same value.

But when we transform an equation, we make another kind of claim.

We're claiming that our reasoning is justified.

And if we're solving an equation through equivalent transformations, we're also claiming that we haven't changed the collection of possible solutions.

These are different promises.

$$
\underbrace{x=2\;\Longrightarrow\;x^2=4}_{\text{Implication}}
$$

$$
\underbrace{x+3=8\;\Longleftrightarrow\;x=5}_{\text{Equivalence}}
$$

A true statement can lead us to another true statement without preserving all the information we started with.

And once we understand that, equations stop being mere collections of symbols to manipulate.

They become statements whose meaning we can examine, whose consequences we can investigate, and whose transformations we can justify.

Which leads us to a deeper question:

When does one statement genuinely follow from another—and how can we prove that the reasoning is valid?

That's where our study of mathematics begins to connect with the foundations of logic.
