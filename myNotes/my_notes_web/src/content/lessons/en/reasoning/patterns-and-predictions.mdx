RATIONAL THINKING · EPISODE 05

## 1. A prediction that feels inevitable

Imagine you're watching a machine.

Every few seconds, it illuminates a collection of small lights. The first time, you see two lights. The second time, four. Then eight.

You continue watching.

<ReasoningFigure kind="pattern-history" />

And then the machine stops.

Not because it has finished, but because someone has paused the recording.

They turn to you and ask:

How many lights will appear next?

Most people will answer 64.

And there's a very good reason for that.

Look at the numbers. Two becomes four. Four becomes eight. Eight becomes sixteen. Sixteen becomes thirty-two.

Every number is twice the previous one.

So it seems natural to say that thirty-two should become sixty-four.

That isn't a random guess. It's a perfectly reasonable prediction.

But imagine the person who asked you the question shakes their head.

And says:

"What if I told you the next number could be thirty-four?"

You might object.

"Thirty-four? That breaks the pattern!"

But think carefully about what you've just said.

Which pattern?

The one you observed?

Or the one you assumed would continue?

Those are not quite the same thing.

And understanding the difference is one of the most important steps toward learning how to reason.

## 2. Two completely different stories

Consider two possible machines.

The first machine has a very simple instruction:

Every round, double the number of lights.

If you start with two lights, you get four, then eight, then sixteen, then thirty-two.

And, naturally, sixty-four.

We could write its rule mathematically as:

$$
\boxed{A(n)=2^n}
$$

Here, $n$ represents the round, starting at $n=1$.

So $A(1)=2$, $A(2)=4$, and $A(6)=64$.

Now consider a second machine.

This machine has two operating modes.

For the first five rounds, it does exactly what the first machine does: it doubles the number of lights.

But after the fifth round, it changes its behavior.

From then on, instead of doubling, it adds two lights every round.

<ReasoningFigure kind="pattern-machines" />

Both machines produce exactly the same five numbers that you saw.

And yet they predict completely different futures.

Here's the important part.

You were never told that the machine must continue doubling forever.

You only observed the first five rounds, containing four doublings between consecutive values.

You supplied the rest.

Now we can express the second machine's behavior as another perfectly well-defined mathematical rule:

$$
B(n)= \begin{cases} 2^n & \text{if }1\le n\le5\\ 32+2(n-5) & \text{if }n\ge6 \end{cases}
$$

It may look more complicated, but its meaning is straightforward. Double during the first five rounds, then add two.

Let's watch what happens when both rules run.

<Interactive kind="patterns" preset="continuations" id="two-continuations" />

Think about what this means.

Before round six, the two explanations are indistinguishable from the information you've been given.

If you saw a hundred rounds of doubling instead of five, you could construct another machine that doubles for exactly a hundred rounds before changing.

And if you saw a million rounds, the same possibility would remain.

No finite list of examples, by itself, guarantees one unique rule for an unrestricted sequence.

This doesn't mean that no rule is correct, or that every rule is equally plausible.

It means the observations alone haven't told us everything we would need to know.

## 3. The three things we must not confuse

Now consider what actually happened when you answered sixty-four.

Your mind performed three distinct operations, so quickly that they felt like just one.

<Flow nodes={[
 {label:"1. Observation",tex:"2,4,8,16,32",detail:"You saw these five numbers: evidence."},
 {label:"2. Proposed rule",tex:"\\times2",detail:"Always multiply by 2: a conjecture."},
 {label:"3. Prediction",tex:"64",detail:"You expected the next term: a forecast."}
]} caption="Observations motivate a rule. The rule produces a prediction. The observations do not, by themselves, establish the rule." />

Notice where the uncertainty enters.

There's nothing uncertain about the first step. Assuming the recording is accurate, the machine really did display those five numbers.

And there's nothing mathematically uncertain about what follows if the first rule is true.

If a number is doubled every round, then thirty-two must become sixty-four.

That's a valid deduction.

The uncertain step comes in the middle.

How do we know that doubling forever is the correct rule?

We don't know that from the five numbers alone.

We have a conjecture: a proposed explanation supported by the evidence, but not established by it.

This is the difference between saying:

"These numbers doubled, so I suspect the next one will be sixty-four."

And saying:

"These numbers doubled, so the next one must be sixty-four."

One is a prediction based on an assumption.

The other claims logical necessity.

And only the first claim is justified by the information we were given.

## 4. But shouldn't we prefer the simplest explanation?

At this point, you might be thinking:

"All right. Technically, there are multiple possible rules. But come on. Surely the doubling rule is better."

And I think that's a very reasonable position.

After all, which explanation would you prefer if you had no other information?

<ReasoningFigure kind="pattern-rules" />

If all we know is the sequence, the first rule looks simpler.

It requires fewer qualifications.

We don't have to invent a particular round at which the machine changes its behavior.

And simplicity is often an extremely useful guide when searching for explanations.

Imagine trying to understand the movements of the planets, the growth of a population, or the behavior of an unfamiliar mathematical function.

If every new observation required a completely unrelated explanation, our theories would quickly become impossibly complicated.

Finding simple relationships helps us organize information, connect observations, and make useful predictions.

But now imagine that someone gives you one more piece of information.

The machine is part of a factory.

During its first five operating rounds, it uses a special doubling mode to increase production.

Afterward, it switches to a maintenance mode that adds two units each round.

Suddenly, which explanation seems more natural?

The second one.

Not because the five observed numbers changed.

They didn't.

What changed was the context.

Same sequence. Different background knowledge.

| No extra information | Known operating modes |
| --- | --- |
| Doubling is a useful simple default. | A planned change after round five becomes plausible. |

So simplicity is a guide, not a guarantee.

The simplest rule to us, given what we know and how we describe the problem, is not necessarily the actual generating process.

And sometimes the most important question isn't "What pattern can I see?"

It's "What information am I missing?"

## 5. The most useful next question

Suppose you could unpause the recording.

You can now observe one more round of the machine.

But before you do, let's try something different.

Instead of asking which rule you prefer, ask:

What observation would help us distinguish the two rules?

We already know that both machines produce the same first five numbers.

So watching the third round again won't help.

Neither will watching the fifth.

We need an observation for which the two rules predict different outcomes.

| Observed cases  | Candidate rule                        | Next test: round 6 |
| --------------- | ------------------------------------- | ------------------ |
| 2, 4, 8, 16, 32 | A: Always double                      | 64                 |
| 2, 4, 8, 16, 32 | B: Double through round 5, then add 2 | 34                 |

Now we have something genuinely useful.

A test.

If the machine displays thirty-four, the always-doubling rule is false.

If it displays sixty-four, the second rule is false.

But there's another twist.

Imagine it displays sixty-four.

Does that prove the first rule is correct forever?

No.

Because perhaps there's a third machine that doubles for six rounds and switches afterward.

The sixth observation might eliminate one of our explanations, but it doesn't eliminate every explanation that could possibly exist.

<ReasoningFigure kind="pattern-test" />

That is a beautiful asymmetry in reasoning.

One incompatible observation can disprove a proposed universal rule.

But one compatible observation doesn't prove that rule universally true.

In fact, this is closely connected to mathematical counterexamples: if someone claims something is true for every case, finding one case where it fails is enough to disprove that claim.

The key lesson isn't that testing is useless.

Quite the opposite.

Testing helps us eliminate incorrect explanations and improve our confidence in the ones that survive.

We simply have to be careful not to confuse surviving a test with being proved true forever.

## 6. The same illusion outside mathematics

Now imagine you're no longer looking at a sequence of lights.

You're looking at the analytics of a YouTube channel.

Over four weeks, its weekly views increase:

<ReasoningFigure kind="pattern-forecasts" />

Look at the first four weeks.

The channel is growing.

Every new week has more views than the previous one.

Naturally, you might expect the growth to continue.

And perhaps it will.

But perhaps those first four weeks coincided with a promotional campaign. Or one especially successful video. Or a temporary increase in recommendations.

Once that situation changes, the views might stop increasing.

Notice that both stories are compatible with the same past observations.

A pattern in past data can give us evidence for a forecast.

But the forecast is not a logical consequence of the data alone.

And the same thing happens in sales, investment returns, housing prices, population growth, and countless other situations.

Sometimes we notice the pattern correctly.

What fools us is quietly assuming that the circumstances producing it will remain unchanged.

The danger isn't recognizing regularities.

The danger is forgetting that a regularity in the observations and a law governing every future observation are different claims.

## 7. Your turn: Can you escape the trap?

Let's finish with a small experiment.

Forget the original machine. You're now given a completely new sequence.

<ReasoningFigure kind="pattern-challenge" />

What comes next?

Don't rush to answer.

Instead, try to do three things in your head.

First, find a simple rule compatible with every number.

Second, invent another rule that also fits every number but produces a different next term.

Third, identify the earliest observation that could distinguish the two.

Take a moment to try.

<Exercise>

One simple rule doubles every time, so the fifth term would be $48$.

Another rule doubles only through round four, then adds three each round. It fits $3,6,12,24$ too, but gives $27$ next.

$$
C(n)=3\cdot2^{n-1}
$$

$$
D(n)=\begin{cases}3\cdot2^{n-1}&1\le n\le4\\24+3(n-4)&n\ge5\end{cases}
$$

The first four terms agree. Round five is the earliest observation that distinguishes these two rules: $48$ versus $27$. Neither rule is forced by the four terms alone.

</Exercise>

One final question. Imagine someone claims:

"Because the first five terms doubled, the sixth term has to be sixty-four."

Which observation would best test the two machine rules we explored earlier?

<Interactive kind="patterns" preset="next-test" id="test-the-two-rules" />

The goal of the exercise isn't to find the most imaginative possible sequence.

It's to develop a habit.

When you discover a pattern, ask what you've actually observed, what rule you've proposed, and what new observation would put that rule to a meaningful test.

This is closely related to the problem-solving philosophy of George Pólya, who emphasized examining a problem from different perspectives, testing approaches, and reconsidering solutions rather than merely producing an answer.



## 8. What have we actually learned?

Return to the original five numbers one last time.

$$
2,\quad4,\quad8,\quad16,\quad32,\quad?
$$

The tempting answer is still sixty-four.

And if someone asked me to make a prediction with no other information, I would probably choose sixty-four too.

The difference is that now we understand what that answer means.

It's the result of a simple proposed rule.

Not a theorem established by the five observations alone.

And that difference matters.

Because recognizing patterns is one of our most powerful intellectual abilities. It allows us to learn from experience, develop scientific theories, and anticipate what might happen next.

But a pattern can also seduce us into certainty that the evidence hasn't earned.

So perhaps the better question is not always:

What comes next?

Sometimes it's:

What would I need to know before I could be sure?

And that question takes us from merely guessing what the world will do to investigating why it behaves the way it does.

## The central idea

Observation tells us what has happened.

Conjecture proposes a rule explaining it.

Prediction tells us what to expect if that rule continues to apply.

Testing helps us distinguish explanations, without automatically proving one true forever.
