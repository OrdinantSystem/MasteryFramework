## 1. The rule that looked impossible to break

Imagine that I tell you I've discovered something remarkable about numbers.

Not just an interesting pattern, but a rule that applies to every number of a certain kind.

Here it is:

<Note title="Our conjecture">

Every positive odd integer greater than one is prime.

</Note>

Let's see why someone might believe this.

Remember that a prime number is a positive integer greater than one that has exactly two positive divisors: one and itself.

We begin with the smallest positive odd integer greater than one.

<ReasoningFigure kind="counter-prime-start" />

Three is prime. Its only positive divisors are 1 and 3.

Five is prime. Its only positive divisors are 1 and 5.

Seven is prime. Again, only 1 and 7.

Three examples, three confirmations. Everything looks perfect.

Now imagine that we continue, finding a thousand examples that support some mathematical claim. We might become extremely confident that we've discovered a universal truth.

But there's a peculiar problem.

No matter how many successful examples we've collected, a single genuine exception is enough to prove that a universal claim is false.

And for our particular rule, we don't even need to search very far.

The next odd integer is nine.

<ReasoningFigure kind="counter-nine" />

There it is.

Nine is odd. Nine is positive. Nine is greater than one.

So nine belongs to the group of numbers our rule was talking about.

And yet nine is not prime.

Our rule has failed.

But why should this one failure carry so much weight? Why doesn't the evidence from all those successful cases balance it out?

To understand that, we need to look at what a universal claim is actually promising.

## 2. The invisible boundary around a claim

Look again at our sentence:

"Every positive odd integer greater than one is prime."

There are actually three important parts hiding inside those words.

First, we're talking about integers: whole numbers, including positive numbers, negative numbers, and zero.

Second, we're selecting particular integers: they must be positive, odd, and greater than one.

Third, we're making a promise about those selected numbers: they must all be prime.

Think of it as a filtering machine.

<ReasoningFigure kind="counter-filter" />

Notice something important.

The rule does not say that every integer is prime. It says that every integer satisfying a particular condition is prime.

And that changes what we should consider evidence against it.

Take the number four.

Four is not prime, because $4=2\times2$.

Does that disprove our statement?

No.

Four is even. Our statement never promised that four would be prime.

It's like saying, "Every book on this shelf is written in English," and then discovering a French book in another room.

The French book might be interesting, but it tells us nothing about the promise we made concerning the shelf.

Nine is different.

Nine is on the shelf, so to speak. It qualifies. And it breaks the promise.

That gives us a general method for identifying counterexamples.

## 3. The two-gate test

A genuine counterexample to a statement of the form "Every object satisfying condition A has property B" must pass two tests.

It must satisfy condition A, and it must fail to have property B.

We can turn those requirements into an interactive machine.

<Interactive kind="counterexamples" preset="prime-detector" id="prime-counterexample" />

Try four, then nine, then fifteen. Notice that four never reaches the second gate as a relevant case, whereas nine and fifteen break the conclusion.

This is the central distinction.

A number can fail to be prime without being a counterexample.

And a number can satisfy the condition without disproving anything.

To overturn the claim, both ingredients must be present simultaneously.

$$
\underbrace{A(x)}_{\text{Condition holds}}\quad\land\quad\underbrace{\neg B(x)}_{\text{Conclusion fails}}
$$

The structure of a counterexample

Once you understand this, a counterexample stops being a mysterious mathematical trick. It becomes a precise demonstration of a broken promise.

## 4. Why one failure is logically decisive

Let's translate our discovery into mathematical language.

We can write the original claim as:

$$
\forall n\in\mathbb Z,\quad \big(n>1\land\operatorname{Odd}(n)\big) \Rightarrow\operatorname{Prime}(n).
$$

It might look complicated, but you've already understood every important part.

The symbol $\forall$ means "for every."

The arrow $\Rightarrow$ means "implies," or in ordinary language, "if ... then ..."

The statement says:

For every integer, if it is odd and greater than one, then it is prime.

Now what would have to be true for this statement to be false?

Exactly what we found: an integer that satisfies the condition but isn't prime.

So the negation is:

$$
\exists n\in\mathbb Z:\quad n>1\land\operatorname{Odd}(n) \land\neg\operatorname{Prime}(n).
$$

The symbol $\exists$ means "there exists."

Look at the transformation.

<ReasoningFigure kind="counter-negation" />

This explains the asymmetry.

Finding a thousand successful examples establishes a thousand particular facts.

Finding one counterexample establishes something much broader: that the universal statement cannot be true.

There isn't a competition between the number of examples on each side.

A universal statement doesn't claim that most cases work. It claims that no exceptions exist.

And the existence of even one exception contradicts that claim.

We can make the logic visible across a whole collection of integers.

## 5. Looking at the whole pattern

Imagine a number board. We begin with the positive integers from 1 to 35.

First, every number is simply a candidate.

Then we apply our condition: odd and greater than one.

Finally, we inspect whether the qualifying numbers are prime.

Explore those three views.

<Interactive kind="counterexamples" preset="sieve" id="number-sieve" />

This visual reveals another lesson.

Our statement was not completely disconnected from reality. Many odd integers greater than one really are prime.

Three, five, seven, eleven, thirteen, seventeen...

The pattern contains genuine successes.

But a statement can contain many true individual cases and still be false as a universal claim.

The issue is not whether the rule is sometimes right.

It's whether it is right every time it is supposed to apply.

And now an interesting question emerges.

If checking examples cannot always establish a universal claim, does that mean checking examples is never a proof?

Not quite.

## 6. When checking every example really is a proof

Suppose we modify our claim.

Instead of saying every positive odd integer greater than one is prime, we say:

"Every odd integer greater than one and less than nine is prime."

Which numbers are we talking about now?

Only three.

<ReasoningFigure kind="counter-finite" />

Three is prime. Five is prime. Seven is prime.

And there are no other odd integers between one and nine.

That means we've checked every single possible case.

So in this finite domain, exhaustive checking is a completely valid mathematical proof.

Compare that with the original rule.

<ReasoningFigure kind="counter-domains" />

This distinction matters enormously.

If you check every entry in a genuinely complete finite table, you can prove a statement about that table.

But if the table represents only a sample from a larger domain, you've established something about the sample—not necessarily about everything beyond it.

Checking a million integers might be an impressive computation. Yet if the claim concerns infinitely many integers, the remaining cases haven't been exhausted.

To prove something about all of them, we need reasoning that encompasses arbitrary cases, like the algebraic arguments we explored in our earlier lesson.

The decisive difference is not how many examples we've checked, but whether we've covered the entire domain.

## 7. Not every kind of claim behaves the same way

Now we can push the idea further.

What happens if we change just one word in our statement?

Instead of saying:

"Every odd integer greater than one is prime."

We say:

"There exists an odd integer greater than one that is prime."

Our original universal statement was false.

But this new existential statement is true.

How do we prove it?

With the number three.

Three is odd, greater than one, and prime.

That's enough.

<ReasoningFigure kind="counter-quantifiers" />

It's almost a mirror image.

To establish that every qualifying case works, you must rule out all possible exceptions.

To establish that at least one works, you need only produce one successful example.

And to disprove that something exists, you must rule out every possible candidate.

But there's a third kind of claim that we encounter constantly outside mathematics: statistical claims.

Imagine that I have ten cards, each either blue or red.

I claim:

"At least eight of these ten cards are blue."

Now suppose you find a red card.

Does that refute my claim?

No. My statement allows up to two red cards.

Try changing the number of red cards below.

<Interactive kind="counterexamples" preset="cards" id="ten-card-claim" />

One red card doesn't disprove the claim.

Two red cards don't disprove it either.

But three red cards do, because at most seven could then be blue.

Notice that we're still using logic. We've simply changed the type of promise.

A universal claim allows zero failures.

An "at least eight out of ten" claim allows two failures.

And a statement such as "This treatment usually works" is different again: we would need to define usually, identify the population, and examine the relevant evidence before deciding whether the claim is supported.

A single genuine failure can overturn a strict universal statement. It doesn't automatically overturn a claim about frequency or probability.

So whenever someone presents evidence, there is an important question we should ask before accepting or rejecting their conclusion:

What exactly did they claim?

## 8. A counterexample can help us discover a better rule

Let's return to nine.

We originally believed every positive odd integer greater than one was prime.

Nine showed that we were wrong.

We could simply abandon the idea.

But perhaps we can learn something from the way it failed.

Nine is not prime because it can be decomposed into smaller factors:

$$
9=3\times3.
$$

The same is true of fifteen:

$$
15=3\times5.
$$

And twenty-five:

$$
25=5\times5.
$$

These aren't random failures. They reveal a structure: a composite odd integer can be expressed as a product of smaller odd integers greater than one.

So perhaps the better question is not whether an odd integer is prime automatically.

Perhaps the question is whether we can identify the factors that prevent it from being prime.

<ReasoningFigure kind="counter-repair" />

That last rule is much more interesting than merely excluding the first number that broke our original claim.

Why does it work?

Imagine a composite odd integer $n$. It can be written as:

$$
n=a\times b
$$

where $a$ and $b$ are odd integers greater than one.

Choose the factors so that $a\leq b$.

Because $a$ is the smaller factor, it cannot be larger than $\sqrt n$. Otherwise both factors would be greater than $\sqrt n$, and their product would be greater than $n$.

So every composite odd integer must have an odd divisor between 3 and its square root.

<ReasoningFigure kind="counter-factors" />

$$
a\leq b\quad\Longrightarrow\quad a^2\leq ab=n
$$

$$
\therefore\quad a\leq\sqrt n
$$

Every composite number has at least one factor no larger than its square root.

Therefore, if we examine an odd integer greater than one and find no odd divisor from 3 up to its square root, the number must be prime.

We've discovered a genuine mathematical criterion.

And notice the difference between what we just did and our first few confirmations.

We are no longer checking a sequence of particular numbers and hoping that the pattern continues.

We've established something about the structure of any composite odd integer.

That's the deeper value of counterexamples.

They do more than show us we're wrong. Sometimes they reveal exactly which assumption was missing, which boundary was too broad, or which relationship we failed to notice.

## 9. Can you find a counterexample yourself?

Before finishing, let's test whether you can transfer this reasoning to a completely different claim.

Consider:

"Every integer divisible by four is also divisible by eight."

Which of the following numbers disproves this statement?

Don't just look for a number that isn't divisible by eight. Remember our two gates.

The number must be divisible by four, and it must fail to be divisible by eight.

<Interactive kind="counterexamples" preset="divisibility" id="divisibility-counterexample" />

Think about both conditions before choosing.

<Exercise>

A complete mathematical response would be:

"The claim is false. Consider the integer 12. Since $12=4\times3$, it is divisible by four. But there is no integer $k$ such that $12=8k$, so it is not divisible by eight. Therefore 12 is a counterexample."

</Exercise>

Notice that we didn't merely announce that the rule was wrong.

We demonstrated exactly how and why it failed.

And that's what separates a mathematical disproof from an unsupported objection.

## 10. What a thousand confirmations cannot promise

We began with an apparently convincing pattern.

Three was prime. Five was prime. Seven was prime.

Every example seemed to support the same rule.

Then came nine.

Nine did not erase the truth of those earlier observations. Three, five, and seven are still prime.

But nine revealed that we had drawn a conclusion larger than our evidence justified.

And this is the essential insight of the lesson.

<ReasoningFigure kind="counter-synthesis" />

And that insight extends beyond mathematics.

Whenever someone says always, never, every, or none, they're making a particularly strong claim.

A genuine counterexample can settle the question.

But when someone says sometimes, at least one, usually, or probably, the logical task changes.

Evidence must be evaluated against the claim that was actually made, not a stronger or weaker version of it.

And perhaps there's one final lesson here.

Finding a counterexample is not merely discovering that we were mistaken.

It is discovering something about the limits of what we knew.

A good counterexample tells us where our understanding stopped being accurate—and sometimes points us toward a better explanation.

So the next time an idea seems supported by every example you've seen, ask yourself:

What would a genuine exception look like?

Would it satisfy all the conditions?

And if I found it, what exactly would it prove?

Because rational thinking isn't just about collecting reasons to believe something.

It's also about knowing what would give us a reason to stop believing it.
