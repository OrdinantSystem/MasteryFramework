## Negating sequence convergence

We first derive failure at one specified target, not the absence of every possible real limit. Keep the sequence and target fixed throughout.

Use the notation from the preceding lesson: $\varepsilon>0$ is tolerance, $N$ the tail threshold and $n$ the test index. Recall convergence to the fixed real target $L$:

$$
\forall\varepsilon>0\;\exists N\in\mathbb N\;\forall n\in\mathbb N\;\bigl(n\ge N\Rightarrow |a_n-L|<\varepsilon\bigr).
$$

Every tolerance needs a threshold whose entire tail passes. The threshold may use the tolerance but cannot change with each later index. The definition imposes no closeness demand before that threshold. The following six stages are equivalent forms of its negation, not yet a proof that a particular sequence fails.


<span id="from-negating-sequence-convergence-1-deny-the-whole-definition"></span>

### 1. Deny the whole definition

$$
\neg\Bigl(\forall\varepsilon>0\;\exists N\in\mathbb N\;\forall n\in\mathbb N\;\bigl(n\ge N\Rightarrow |a_n-L|<\varepsilon\bigr)\Bigr).
$$

At this stage put negation outside the complete statement and leave every internal symbol unchanged. The fixed context remains the same.


<span id="from-negating-sequence-convergence-2-cross-the-tolerance-quantifier"></span>

### 2. Cross the tolerance quantifier

$$
\exists\varepsilon>0\;\neg\Bigl(\exists N\in\mathbb N\;\forall n\in\mathbb N\;\bigl(n\ge N\Rightarrow |a_n-L|<\varepsilon\bigr)\Bigr).
$$

Not every positive tolerance works, so some positive tolerance defeats the remaining requirement. Positivity is retained; we do not switch to nonpositive tolerances.


<span id="from-negating-sequence-convergence-3-cross-the-threshold-quantifier"></span>

### 3. Cross the threshold quantifier

$$
\exists\varepsilon>0\;\forall N\in\mathbb N\;\neg\Bigl(\forall n\in\mathbb N\;\bigl(n\ge N\Rightarrow |a_n-L|<\varepsilon\bigr)\Bigr).
$$

For that one tolerance no natural threshold works. Therefore every proposed threshold fails the complete inner index claim. Keep the tolerance fixed while thresholds vary.


<span id="from-negating-sequence-convergence-4-cross-the-test-index-quantifier"></span>

### 4. Cross the test-index quantifier

$$
\exists\varepsilon>0\;\forall N\in\mathbb N\;\exists n\in\mathbb N\;\neg\bigl(n\ge N\Rightarrow |a_n-L|<\varepsilon\bigr).
$$

Failure at every index is unnecessary. For each threshold, one index must make the conditional false. The index may depend on that threshold and the already fixed context.


<span id="from-negating-sequence-convergence-5-deny-the-implication"></span>

### 5. Deny the implication

$$
\exists\varepsilon>0\;\forall N\in\mathbb N\;\exists n\in\mathbb N\;\bigl(n\ge N\land\neg(|a_n-L|<\varepsilon)\bigr).
$$

A false implication has true hypothesis and false conclusion. The same index must lie in the tail and fail the distance test. Keep the hypothesis comparison unchanged and replace the implication by conjunction. A pre-tail term cannot break a tail promise.


<span id="from-negating-sequence-convergence-6-include-equality-in-distance-failure"></span>

### 6. Include equality in distance failure

$$
\exists\varepsilon>0\;\forall N\in\mathbb N\;\exists n\in\mathbb N\;\bigl(n\ge N\land |a_n-L|\ge\varepsilon\bigr).
$$

A real distance not below $\varepsilon$ is at least $\varepsilon$. This is the literal comparison complement at the same tolerance, so equality must appear. Read the result: choose one positive tolerance; against every threshold, find a term in its tail at least that far from $L$. “Arbitrarily far out” means at or beyond every proposed threshold, not that every late term fails.


<span id="from-negating-sequence-convergence-prove-one-actual-failure"></span>

### Prove one actual failure

For $a_n=0$ at all indices and $L=1$, choose $\varepsilon=1$. For any natural $N$, choose $n=N$. Membership and $n\ge N$ hold, and $|a_n-1|=1\ge\varepsilon$. This covers every threshold and proves failure at one. For target zero, by contrast, any $\varepsilon>0$ works with $N=1$: every distance is zero. Thus this sequence has a real limit despite failing at target one.

## No real limit

The example above shows why one failed target is not enough. No real limit requires failure for every candidate real target, with the sequence fixed as targets vary.

Write $C(L)$ for the complete positive convergence definition for this fixed sequence and target $L$. This is an abbreviation, not a new definition. Having a real limit means $\exists L\in\mathbb R\;C(L)$. Negate this outer existence claim:

$$
\neg(\exists L\in\mathbb R\;C(L))\ \Longleftrightarrow\ \forall L\in\mathbb R\;\neg C(L).
$$

For each target substitute the failure condition already derived. The target comes before the tolerance, so the tolerance may depend on the target. It must still remain fixed against all thresholds for that target.

$$
\forall L\in\mathbb R\;\exists\varepsilon>0\;\forall N\in\mathbb N\;\exists n\in\mathbb N\;\bigl(n\ge N\land |a_n-L|\ge\varepsilon\bigr).
$$

This says no finite real limit exists, sometimes called divergence as a real sequence. It does not say the terms grow or tend to infinity. It also does not demand one tolerance working for all targets: moving the tolerance in front of the target would be a stronger requirement needing its own justification. The violating index may use both target and threshold, as well as the chosen tolerance and fixed sequence.


<span id="from-no-real-limit-a-quantifier-swap-can-destroy-the-test"></span>

### A quantifier swap can destroy the test

$$
\forall N\in\mathbb N\;\exists\varepsilon>0\;\exists n\in\mathbb N\;\bigl(n\ge N\land |a_n-L|>\varepsilon\bigr).
$$

This proposed direct negation has two unjustified changes. It lets the tolerance be selected anew after each threshold, rather than selecting one tolerance defeating every threshold. And it replaces strict less than by strict greater than at the same tolerance, omitting equality. Negation changes binder types in place; it does not perform that swap.

An optional countermodel to the swapped order uses the [reciprocal result](../reading-sequence-convergence/#from-reciprocal-sequence-convergence), with its explicitly accepted Archimedean prerequisite. At target zero, $a_n=1/n$ converges, yet the displayed wrong-order condition holds: for each $N$, choose $n=N$ and $\varepsilon=1/(2N)$. Then $1/N>1/(2N)>0$. This countermodel is optional; the main negation route needs only the constant-sequence proofs.


<span id="from-no-real-limit-optional-nuance-a-separately-equivalent-strict-form"></span>

### Optional nuance: a separately equivalent strict form

With the correct order $\exists\varepsilon>0\;\forall N\;\exists n$, a strict-distance version is in fact equivalent to failure. If distance at least $\varepsilon$ occurs in every tail, choose the smaller positive tolerance $\varepsilon/2$. The same bad terms have distance strictly greater than that half. Conversely, distance greater than a positive tolerance implies distance at least that tolerance. This proves equivalence by changing the tolerance, not by claiming that $\neg(d<\varepsilon)$ means $d>\varepsilon$ at a fixed value. Halving cannot repair the quantifier swap.
