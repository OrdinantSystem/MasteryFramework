## Negating nested statements

“Every student can read a shelf book” fails when one student cannot read any shelf book. “A shelf book can be read by every student” fails when each proposed book has a student who cannot read it. That student may vary with the book. Separate reading successes need not supply a shared book.


<span id="from-negating-nested-statements-work-outside-in-on-a-finite-domain"></span>

### Work outside in on a finite domain

Let $D=\{1,2\}$. The claim $\forall x\in D\;\exists y\in D\;(y>x)$ fails at $x=2$: neither allowed $y$ is larger. Derive that failure, keeping the whole inner existence claim together.

$$
\begin{aligned}\neg(\forall x\in D\;\exists y\in D\;(y>x))&\Longleftrightarrow\exists x\in D\;\neg(\exists y\in D\;(y>x))\\&\Longleftrightarrow\exists x\in D\;\forall y\in D\;\neg(y>x)\\&\Longleftrightarrow\exists x\in D\;\forall y\in D\;(y\le x).\end{aligned}
$$

The first step denies every input by selecting one failing input. The second says no witness works for that input, so every candidate fails. The last includes equality in not greater. Choose two once and keep it fixed while testing one and two. Switching the binder types in place does not reverse their order or exchange relation arguments.


<span id="from-negating-nested-statements-start-with-existence-instead"></span>

### Start with existence instead

$$
\neg(\exists x\in D\;\forall y\in D\;(y<x))\ \Longleftrightarrow\ \forall x\in D\;\exists y\in D\;(y\ge x).
$$

The original asks for an $x$ strictly exceeding every member, including itself; it is not the usual non-strict maximum condition. For any proposed $x$, the test $y=x$ defeats it. The denial is true with that same allowed value as $y$. On an empty domain the original is false and the denial vacuously true; no witness must be supplied for an outer universal with no cases.

Changing the first finite domain to all reals makes the original larger-member claim true by adding one; its correct negation becomes false. The logical pattern is unchanged, but truth depends on the domain. Correct symbolic negation is not a proof that failure occurs.

## Negating quantified statements step by step

“Not every applicant has a document passing every check” says that some applicant has no document passing them all. For that fixed applicant, every document fails at least one check. Changing three quantifier words together can hide which object stays fixed. Follow the parentheses from the outside inward.

Let $A$ be applicants, $B$ documents, $C$ checks. Let $R(x,y,z)$ mean document $y$ passes check $z$ for applicant $x$. All three domains are fixed here. Begin with the full negation, not an edited unnegated claim:

$$
\begin{aligned}&\neg(\forall x\in A\;\exists y\in B\;\forall z\in C\;R(x,y,z))\\\Longleftrightarrow\;&\exists x\in A\;\neg(\exists y\in B\;\forall z\in C\;R(x,y,z))\\\Longleftrightarrow\;&\exists x\in A\;\forall y\in B\;\neg(\forall z\in C\;R(x,y,z))\\\Longleftrightarrow\;&\exists x\in A\;\forall y\in B\;\exists z\in C\;\neg R(x,y,z).\end{aligned}
$$

The first dual law supplies a failing applicant; the next denies existence of a successful document for that applicant; the final law supplies a failing check for each document. The applicant stays fixed. The check may depend on the document. No one check needs to defeat all documents.


<span id="from-negating-quantified-statements-step-by-step-why-each-replacement-is-legitimate"></span>

### Why each replacement is legitimate

Treat the whole inner claim as one predicate of the current variable. The dual law preserves its truth value for every assignment of already fixed outer variables. Therefore leaving the surrounding quantifiers unchanged preserves their truth values too. Repeating this gives an equivalence chain. All its lines can be false: it identifies failure without proving failure occurs. To prove the original false, establish the final formula separately.


<span id="from-negating-quantified-statements-step-by-step-a-method-that-respects-scope"></span>

### A method that respects scope

1. Parenthesize the exact claim being denied.
2. Record the ordered variables, their domains and the fixed parameters.
3. Cross the next quantifier only: switch its type and negate the whole remainder.
4. Repeat, preserving positions and domains.
5. At a connective, apply its own rule before proceeding into the affected parts. Negate the final predicate too.
6. Read back the result and audit which witnesses may use which earlier information.

Do not flip quantifiers outside the negation’s actual scope. A free variable remains free. A shelf-dependent book domain stays attached to its shelf. “Positive real” remains positive real. These checks prevent a memorized prefix trick from silently changing the question.

$$
\neg(A\Rightarrow B)\ \Longleftrightarrow\ A\land\neg B.
$$

A signed document fails the promise “if signed, then passes” exactly when it is signed and does not pass. An unsigned document cannot refute the implication. This is why the original hypothesis remains true in a counterexample.
