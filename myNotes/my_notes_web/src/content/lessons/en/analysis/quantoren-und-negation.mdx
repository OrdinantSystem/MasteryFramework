## A witness versus every case

This page is a compact reference. For a first explanation, begin with [universal statements and their domains](../universal-statements-and-domains/) and [existence and witnesses](../existence-and-witnesses/). Then work through [bound variables and scope](../bound-variables-and-scope/), [quantifier order](../quantifier-order/) and [negating universal claims](../negating-universal-statements/). Those lessons develop the examples and proofs behind the formulas collected here.

Existence needs one witness. A universal claim covers every element of its stated domain; one counterexample refutes it. $\exists!$ means exactly one, combining existence and uniqueness.

<Math tex="\exists x\in\mathbb N:x+3=10\quad\text{(}x=7\text{)}\qquad\neg\bigl(\forall x\in\mathbb N:x+3=10\bigr)" display />

The left-hand claim is true because $7$ belongs to the domain and satisfies the equation. The universal claim inside the negation is false: for example, $x=1$ gives $1+3=4$, not $10$. Thus both displayed claims are true, but for different reasons. One successful input establishes existence; a different, unsuccessful input refutes universality.

## Push the negation inward

<Math tex="\neg(\forall x\in D:P(x))\iff\exists x\in D:\neg P(x)" display />

<Math tex="\neg(\exists x\in D:P(x))\iff\forall x\in D:\neg P(x)" display />

Negation changes the quantifier and the predicate, while keeping the domain. With nested quantifiers, change each one in order. Their order matters: one common $y$ for every $x$ is stronger than a possibly different $y$ for each $x$.

<Math tex="\neg(\forall x\,\exists y:P(x,y))\iff\exists x\,\forall y:\neg P(x,y)" display />

## The boundary case matters

The claim $n^2 > n$ for every natural number fails at $n = 1$. With $\mathbb{N}$ starting at 1, the correct universal statement is $n^2 \ge  n$; strict inequality holds for $n > 1$.

## Order of quantifiers

Over $\mathbb{N}$, “for every $x$ there is a $y$ greater than $x$” is true: after seeing $x$, choose $y = x + 1$. “There is a $y$ greater than every $x$” is false: once $y$ is fixed, $x = y$ defeats the claim. In the first statement $y$ may depend on $x$; in the second the same $y$ must work throughout. To negate the first, say that some $x$ has no greater natural $y$. This changes both quantifiers, not just the final inequality.

<Exercise question="Negate: every natural number is even.">

Some natural number is not even; 1 is a witness. Saying every natural number is odd would be a stronger and false claim.

</Exercise>
