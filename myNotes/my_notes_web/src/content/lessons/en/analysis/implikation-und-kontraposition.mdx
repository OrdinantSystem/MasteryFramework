## Material implication and vacuity

For real $x$, test “above two implies above five”: seven satisfies both, three only the premise, zero neither. Classical logic evaluates even that last conditional.


<span id="from-material-implication-and-vacuity-the-definition-includes-four-cases"></span>

### The definition includes four cases

For meaningful $P,Q$, **material implication** $P\Rightarrow Q$ (“if P, then Q”) is false exactly at true $P$, false $Q$. Here $1$ and $0$ mean true/false:

| $P$ | $Q$ | $P\Rightarrow Q$ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $1$ | $1$ | $1$ |
| $1$ | $0$ | $0$ |
| $0$ | $1$ | $1$ |
| $0$ | $0$ | $1$ |

This defines the classical connective. Unknown is no fifth case: hidden cards have fixed numbers. The arrow asserts neither component, conjunction nor causation.

From the definition:

$$(P\Rightarrow Q)\equiv(\neg P\lor Q).$$

“If P then Q” equals “not P or Q,” inclusively. $\equiv$ means agreement under every assignment: false $P$ makes $\neg P$ and OR true regardless of $Q$; true $P$ makes OR take $Q$’s value. Both cases prove the result.


<span id="from-material-implication-and-vacuity-what-vacuous-truth-does-and-does-not-say"></span>

### What vacuous truth does and does not say

False-premise conditionals are **vacuously true**. For above-five-to-above-two, zero has false conclusion and three true conclusion; both conditionals hold. The conclusion is undetermined.

A box containing exactly zero and three obeys “every card above five exceeds two”: its domain is nonempty, its eligible subset empty. An empty box has empty domain. Neither supplies a card above five. The rule implies existence neither above five nor above two; contents, not the rule, establish the latter here. Truth, existence and usefulness differ.

For **above-two-to-above-five**, zero gives true, three false. Restricting to reals at most two makes every premise false and the rule universally true there, but changes the all-real claim.


<span id="from-material-implication-and-vacuity-one-assignment-is-not-logical-consequence"></span>

### One assignment is not logical consequence

For $P(x):x>2$, $Q(x):x>5$, real order prevents false/true. Other propositions realize it, such as $P:(0=1)$, $Q:(2=2)$, so the general table needs that row.

**Logical consequence** excludes every permitted assignment/model with true premises and false conclusion. One true conditional assignment proves neither unrestricted consequence nor universality over numerical inputs. Domain-wide arithmetic proofs also use arithmetic assumptions.


<span id="from-material-implication-and-vacuity-meaning-comes-before-the-table"></span>

### Meaning comes before the table

A false premise cannot make $1/0=2$ meaningful in real arithmetic. Explicitly exclude zero for reciprocals. Guarded informal notation needs an agreed interpretation; implication alone is not programming short-circuit evaluation that repairs partial operations.

<Exercise question="Evaluate $(x>2)\Rightarrow(x>5)$ at $7,3,0$. Does zero prove an all-real theorem? Realize false/true with other propositions.">

Pairs true/true, true/false, false/false give $1,0,1$. Three refutes universality despite zero. These comparisons cannot realize false/true, but “zero equals one” and “two equals two” do; the definition covers them too.

</Exercise>

<Exercise question="A box obeys above-five-implies-above-two with no card above five. Must all cards be at most two? Compare empty, zero-only and three-only boxes.">

No: all obey the rule, with no above-five card. Empty has no cards; zero is at most two; three exceeds two. Neither the rule nor absence of eligible cards establishes existence or decides whether any exceed two.

</Exercise>

<span id="from-reading-conditionals"></span>

## Reading conditionals

“A card exceeds two **if** it exceeds five” still runs above-five to above-two: if introduces the premise even when last.


<span id="from-reading-conditionals-hypothesis-and-conclusion"></span>

### Hypothesis and conclusion

In $P\Rightarrow Q$, the whole $P$ is **hypothesis/antecedent**, the whole $Q$ **conclusion/consequent**. Roles assert no truth: even false “if three exceeds two, then three exceeds five” has an identifiable conclusion.

For fixed real $x$:

$$((x>0)\land(x<1))\Rightarrow(x<2).$$

“If x is positive and below one, it is below two.” The outer arrow joins the whole **two-bound** hypothesis to the conclusion. Reals form the domain; the hypothesis selects inputs between zero and one.

One half passes both; negative one only the upper bound, two only the lower. Neither latter input is eligible. The arrow binds no $x$ and adds no universal quantifier; context fixes one-versus-all inputs.


<span id="from-reading-conditionals-keep-the-stated-assumptions"></span>

### Keep the stated assumptions

For arbitrary eligible real $x$, $x<1$ and $1<2$ give $x<2$ by ordinary order transitivity.

Unused $x>0$ still belongs to the stated theorem. Removing it strengthens the claim to more inputs, true here by the same proof, but not the original quotation. Adding assumptions generally eases conditional validity; removing them broadens its reach.


<span id="from-reading-conditionals-prove-the-whole-target"></span>

### Prove the whole target

A two-part conclusion:

$$(x>5)\Rightarrow((x>2)\land(x>0)).$$

“Above five implies above two and above zero.” Order gives $x>2$, then $x>0$ since $2>0$. Prove both; inclusive OR would need one alternative.

For target $x<2$, $x\le2$ leaves equality unresolved; stronger $x<1$ suffices by $x<1<2$.


<span id="from-reading-conditionals-applying-a-rule-needs-two-premises"></span>

### Applying a rule needs two premises

Knowing the rule and that a hidden card exceeds five gives above two without knowing its exact value. **Modus ponens** uses both $P\Rightarrow Q$ and $P$ to infer $Q$.

Otherwise true $P$ and false $Q$ would falsify the assumed rule. Rule alone fails at card zero; conclusion alone cannot recover the premise, as three exceeds two but not five.

<Exercise question="For real x, parse $((x>1)\land(x<4))\Rightarrow(x>0)$. Name complete hypothesis/conclusion; test eligibility of $0,2,4$; prove for arbitrary input.">

Hypothesis: $x>1$ **and** $x<4$; conclusion: $x>0$. Only two is eligible: zero fails the lower bound, four the strict upper bound despite true conclusion. Arbitrary eligible $x$ satisfies $x>1>0$ by transitivity. The unused upper bound remains stated.

</Exercise>

<Exercise question="A proof targeting $x<2$ stops at $x\le2$. What remains? Invent a two-part hypothesis with one unused assumption; explain its removal.">

Exclude $x=2$, allowed by the weaker bound; this does not say it meets the original hypotheses. Example: $1<x<4$ implies $x<5$ by upper bound and transitivity. Removing $x>1$ includes negative reals below four: a stronger theorem, also proved by transitivity.

</Exercise>

## A conditional and its negation

<span id="from-logische-verknuepfungen-negating-an-implication"></span>

### Negating an implication

<Math tex="\neg(\colA{A}\Rightarrow \colB{B})\iff \colA{A}\land\neg \colB{B}" display />

Denial selects true $A$, false $B$: refuting “if I pass, I buy dinner” requires passing without buying. Negating both selects another case.


<span id="from-logische-verknuepfungen-reading-an-implication"></span>

**Both** directions require $A\Leftrightarrow B$, not just $A\Rightarrow B$. Below, odd inputs cannot refute the divisibility rule’s forward direction.

<span id="from-implikation-und-kontraposition"></span>

## Implication and contraposition

<span id="from-implikation-und-kontraposition-change-the-statement-then-test-all-cases"></span>

### Change the statement, then test all cases

Read $A\Rightarrow B$ as “if A then B.” Transform complete blocks, even compound ones, without changing domain:

| Form | Formula | Reading and construction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Original | $A\Rightarrow B$ | If $A$, then $B$ |
| Converse | $B\Rightarrow A$ | If $B$, then $A$: exchange the blocks |
| Inverse | $\neg A\Rightarrow\neg B$ | If not $A$, then not $B$: negate without exchanging |
| Contrapositive | $\neg B\Rightarrow\neg A$ | If not $B$, then not $A$: exchange and negate |

These transformations define statements, not prove them. Inverse is not denial: $\neg(A\Rightarrow B)$ is $A\land\neg B$.

Reals above five exceed two by transitivity. Converse fails at three; inverse (at most five implies at most two) at three and five, retaining equality. Contrapositive (at most two implies at most five) holds by order. Some converses/inverses hold: $x+1=3$ implies $x=2$ by subtraction, converse by addition, inverse because $x=2$ would force the equality. Not generally equivalent does not mean always different.

Vary the activity; compare complete columns, not one row.

<Interactive kind="logic" preset="implication" id="implication-lab" display />

All four rows matter: original agrees with contrapositive; converse agrees with inverse, not necessarily original.

<Math tex="(\colA{A}\Rightarrow \colB{B})\iff(\neg \colB{B}\Rightarrow\neg \colA{A})\qquad(\colB{B}\Rightarrow \colA{A})\iff(\neg \colA{A}\Rightarrow\neg \colB{B})" display />


<span id="from-implikation-und-kontraposition-why-the-paired-forms-agree"></span>

### Why the paired forms agree

Original fails only at true $A$, false $B$. Contrapositive fails at true $\neg B$, false $\neg A$: the same row. Both hold elsewhere, deriving equivalence even for false originals. Converse and inverse both fail only at false $A$, true $B$, deriving their agreement too.

Thus prove a domain-wide conditional by proving its contrapositive in the same domain. For real $x^2\ne0\Rightarrow x\ne0$, prove $x=0\Rightarrow x^2=0$ by substitution and zero multiplication, not merely by writing it.

For $(0<x<1)\Rightarrow x<2$: converse $x<2\Rightarrow(0<x<1)$; inverse $((x\le0)\lor(x\ge1))\Rightarrow x\ge2$; contrapositive $x\ge2\Rightarrow((x\le0)\lor(x\ge1))$. Deny entire intervals by at least one failed bound, not both. Negating a conjunctive conclusion likewise produces an OR of failures.

For $(x>5)\Rightarrow((x>2)\land(x>0))$, contraposition gives $((x\le2)\lor(x\le0))\Rightarrow(x\le5)$: “at most two or at most zero implies at most five.” Either alternative proves the target by order for every real. Requiring both failures changes the hypothesis.

Inferring $A$ from $B$ and the rule **affirms the consequent**; inferring $\neg B$ from $\neg A$ **denies the antecedent**. Three defeats the first; zero and three show false premises allow either conclusion.

<Exercise question="Over reals, transform $((x>1)\land(x<4))\Rightarrow(x>0)$ into converse, inverse and contrapositive. Test converse at one; prove contrapositive. Identify the common abstract false row.">

Converse: $x>0\Rightarrow((x>1)\land(x<4))$, false at one since $1>1$ fails. Inverse: $((x\le1)\lor(x\ge4))\Rightarrow x\le0$. Contrapositive: $x\le0\Rightarrow((x\le1)\lor(x\ge4))$, proved by $x\le0\le1$. Common abstract failure is true $A$, false $B$, hence true $\neg B$, false $\neg A$; the only failure for either form proves agreement.

</Exercise>


<span id="from-implikation-und-kontraposition-divisibility-gives-a-concrete-example"></span>

### Divisibility gives a concrete example

$a | n$ means $n=ak$ for integer $k$: divisibility, not division. Prove $4 | n$ implies $2 | n$; compare contrapositive and converse.


<span id="from-implikation-und-kontraposition-a-proof-and-its-converse"></span>

### A proof and its converse

If $n=4k$ for integer $k$, then $n=2(2k)$ with integer $2k$: two divides $n$ for every integer input. Contraposition says odd $n$ cannot be a multiple of four. Evenness need not imply divisibility by four: six gives false $A$, true $B$, true original but false converse.

<Exercise question="Does $3 | n$ imply $6 | n$? Give a counterexample and state the contrapositive.">

No: $n=3$. Contrapositive: “if six does not divide n, three does not divide n,” also false at three. Contraposition preserves falsity too.

</Exercise>

<span id="from-implication-and-model-assumptions"></span>

## Implication and model assumptions

Seven exceeds five and two without changing; comparisons describe properties, not causes.

Material implication is **truth-functional**, determined by component values, with no extra column for time, explanation or cause. In ordinary arithmetic,

$$(0=1)\Rightarrow(2=3)$$

is true from false premise: “If zero equals one, two equals three.” Both parts remain false; the arrow proves neither and supplies no cause.


<span id="from-implication-and-model-assumptions-an-exact-rule-inside-a-hypothetical-model"></span>

### An exact rule inside a hypothetical model

Stipulate a hypothetical device rule for every considered state: **closed switch implies lit lamp**. This assumption is not verified real equipment behavior. $S$ means switch closed; $L$, lamp lit.

| Given within the model | Licensed conclusion | Reason |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $S\Rightarrow L$ and $S$ | $L$ | Modus ponens |
| $S\Rightarrow L$ and $\neg L$ | $\neg S$ | Contraposition |
| $S\Rightarrow L$ and $L$ | No conclusion about $S$ | The converse was not supplied |
| $S\Rightarrow L$ and $\neg S$ | No conclusion about $L$ | The inverse was not supplied |

$\neg L\Rightarrow\neg S$ reads “unlit lamp implies switch not closed”: states consistent with the rule, not darkness opening switches or reversing causes.

False $S$, true $L$ obeys the rule: light alone cannot force a closed switch. Familiarity supplies no missing model assumption.


<span id="from-implication-and-model-assumptions-deduction-and-observation-answer-different-questions"></span>

### Deduction and observation answer different questions

Evaluation checks one pair for true/false failure; theorem proof excludes that pair for every allowed input (above five entails above two by order transitivity). Empirical support checks assumptions against system and evidence. Correct model deductions do not validate the model.

Accurate closed-switch/unlit-lamp observation contradicts the exact rule in its intended setting. They cannot both hold. Logic alone diagnoses neither broken components, missing conditions nor causes; investigate further.

<Exercise question="Given only $S\Rightarrow L$ and reported light, refute ‘switch closed’ with a consistent state. What changes with reported closed switch and unlit lamp?">

False $S$, true $L$ satisfies rule and report but defeats backward inference. True $S$, false $L$ refutes the exact rule if descriptions are accurate, without diagnosing equipment. Adding converse licenses new inference but adds an assumption needing support.

</Exercise>
