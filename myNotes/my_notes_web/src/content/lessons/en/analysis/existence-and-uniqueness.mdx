## Existence and witnesses

<span id="from-existence-and-witnesses-candidate-membership-whole-condition"></span>

### Candidate, membership, whole condition

Seven witnesses $\exists n\in\mathbb Z\;(n+5=12)$: it is integer and $7+5=12$. Witness means object, verification means argument. For $4<n<8$, five passes both bounds; nine only the lower, three only the upper. Repeated variables require one object.

The club needs Tuesday availability **and** first-aid training in one member. Ada’s availability plus Ben’s training fails. Cora needs both and membership; outsiders fail even with both.

Real $1/2$ solves $2x=1$, but integers are at most zero or at least one, their doubles at most zero or at least two. Likewise $0<x<1$ admits $1/2$, no integer. These exhaustive arguments refute existence; finite searches do so only when exhaustive.

## Constructing a witness

Twelve minus five discovers seven; verification follows separately.

<Math tex="\exists n\in\mathbb Z\;(n+5=12)." display />


<span id="from-constructing-a-witness-a-complete-direct-proof"></span>

### A complete direct proof

Choose $n=7$; verify $7\in\mathbb Z$ and $7+5=12$; conclude existence. Candidate, membership, full predicate, conclusion: one success suffices, without uniqueness.

Constructive proofs produce and check an object; classical existence proofs need not name one. “Choose an integer that works” circularly assumes success. Name a known integer, then verify.


<span id="from-constructing-a-witness-backward-work-and-domain-checks"></span>

### Backward work and domain checks

Working backward from $2x=1$ forces $x=1/2$: real membership and substitution prove real, not integer existence. Necessary candidates alone do not suffice; reversible algebra with domain checks can. Backward reasoning is legitimate, but requested false claims remain unprovable.

For $4<n<8$, five, six, seven work; four fails strictly. For arbitrary real $x$, closure makes $x+1$ real, positive one makes it larger. Testing zero is not general proof.

<span id="from-existence-and-uniqueness"></span>

## Existence and uniqueness

Fix allowed keys. One opening key proves existence; a distinct second preserves existence but refutes uniqueness.


<span id="from-existence-and-uniqueness-three-different-requirements"></span>

### Three different requirements

Existence: at least one success. At most one: no two distinct successes, allowing zero. Unique existence combines both. Empty drawer: false, true, false; excluding a second supplies no first.

$$
\exists! x\in D\;P(x).
$$

“!” adds at-most-one, not emphasis. Verify a witness in $D$ with $P$, then show every allowed solution equals it, not nonsolutions. $1/2$ and $2/4$ name one object despite different spellings.


<span id="from-existence-and-uniqueness-a-proof-with-both-obligations"></span>

### A proof with both obligations

$$
\exists! n\in\mathbb Z\;(n+5=12).
$$

Existence is proved above. Subtract five from any integer solution $n+5=12$: $n=7$, equal to our witness. This covers arbitrary solutions, not arbitrary integers or failed samples.

Integers above ten exclude forced candidate seven: at-most-one true, existence false. For $4<n<8$, distinct five and six refute uniqueness without enumeration.

## Counting witnesses

Seven and nine both witness oddness; choosing seven leaves nine. Listing all interval witnesses additionally requires exclusion.

One checked object proves existence; two distinct ones refute at-most-one, not prove exactly two. Complete lists verify entries and exclude all others. Infinite domains admit zero, one, finitely or infinitely many successes.

$$
\{n\in\mathbb Z:4<n<8\}=\{5,6,7\}.
$$

Listed integers pass both bounds. Conversely, integer spacing forces at least five and at most seven: exactly five, six, seven. This exclusion, not three successes alone, proves completeness.


<span id="from-counting-witnesses-changing-the-domain-changes-the-count"></span>

### Changing the domain changes the count

Reals $5.5$, $6.25$ also work. For infinitely many witnesses, insert the midpoint of five and six, then repeatedly of five and the latest value. Each is real, above five, below its predecessor, hence inside the interval and distinct from all earlier values. Another always exists by midpoint arithmetic and order, without convergence.

$1/2=2/4$ is one value; two physical seven-cards are distinct cards with one label. Specify the objects counted. Empty trays lack candidates, even-only trays lack odd successes: both refute odd existence. Empty universality stays true without witnesses.
