## Universal statements and domains

A box holds exactly two, four and six: every card is positive. Adding zero refutes “every”, though most remain positive; zero outside the box is irrelevant.


<span id="from-universal-statements-and-domains-domain-predicate-quantifier"></span>

### Domain, predicate, quantifier

The **domain** $D$ specifies allowed objects; the **predicate** $P$ is a condition meaningful at each allowed object. Fixing other parameters, write

$$\forall x\in D\;P(x).$$

Read “every $x$ in $D$ satisfies $P$”. $\forall$ means every, $\in$ membership, and $P(x)$ applies a predicate, not multiplication. Universal truth requires every member to pass; one allowed failure refutes it. Not every universal statement is true.

$\forall x\in\mathbb R\;(x>0)$ is false at zero. Reals $\mathbb R$ include zero, negatives and fractions. Positivity holds on positive integers, not all integers $\mathbb Z$; minus one half cannot refute an integer claim.


<span id="from-universal-statements-and-domains-defined-false-or-outside-the-claim"></span>

### Defined, false, or outside the claim?

Distinguish an out-of-domain noninteger from the defined but false $x>0$ at zero. There, $1/x$ is undefined, not an ordinary false equality or third truth value.

Repair the domain explicitly:

$$\forall x\in\mathbb R\setminus\{0\}\;(x\cdot(1/x)=1).$$

Removing zero makes every real times its reciprocal equal one. Under our explicit-domain convention, a false hypothesis cannot give an undefined expression a value; informal guarded notation requires an agreed interpretation.

A hypothesis selects a subset only when all predicates are defined on the ambient domain:

$$\forall x\in\mathbb R\;((x>5)\Rightarrow(x>2)).$$

Every real above five exceeds two by transitivity. For other reals the hypothesis is false, making the conditional true. These cases cover all reals, not just the selected subset.


<span id="from-universal-statements-and-domains-empty-collections-and-changed-domains"></span>

### Empty collections and changed domains

“Every card is red” holds in an empty box: no nonred card exists, but neither does a red witness. The room still contains box and table; an empty restricted domain does not require an empty overall universe.

Restricting the domain of a true universal statement preserves truth, including restriction to the empty set; enlarging it may add a failure, as zero did. Restricting a false all-real claim may produce a true new statement, not prove the original.


<span id="from-universal-statements-and-domains-what-proves-an-infinite-domain-claim"></span>

### What proves an infinite-domain claim?

Exhaustive finite checks prove universality; finite samples cannot cover all reals. An argument must cover an arbitrary allowed input without extra assumptions, not call it arbitrary then choose seven. For the complete adding-two proof, see [Proof methods](../beweissprache-und-methoden/#from-beweissprache-und-methoden).

In $\forall x\in\mathbb R\;(x+a>x)$, $a$ stays free: $a=2$ makes it true, $a=0$ false. Assignment enables evaluation, not binding; see [scope](../universal-statements-and-domains/#from-bound-variables-and-scope).

For possibly empty collections, either separate empty and nonempty cases or assume an arbitrary symbol belongs and discharge that assumption. Neither method asserts a member exists.

<Exercise question="For a box containing exactly $2$, $4$, $6$, explain exhaustive checking versus three real-number checks, adding zero, and not-all-positive.">

The three positive cards exhaust the box, not the reals. Adding zero refutes universality. Not all positive means at least one failure, not none positive: the original three still pass.

</Exercise>

<Exercise question="Classify minus one half against an integer claim, zero against universal real positivity, and zero in an all-real reciprocal formula. Prove every real above eight exceeds three, including false hypotheses.">

Minus one half is outside the domain; zero refutes defined positivity but makes its reciprocal undefined, requiring domain repair. For arbitrary real $x$, $x>8$ implies $x>3$ by $8>3$ and transitivity. Otherwise the false hypothesis makes the conditional true. All reals are covered without asserting an eligible input exists.

</Exercise>

<span id="from-existence-and-witnesses"></span>

## Existence and witnesses

Among cards two, four and seven, seven witnesses an odd card. Two and four do not refute existence; adding another success, nine, preserves it.

$$
\exists x\in D\;P(x).
$$

$\exists$ means at least one member of $D$ satisfies $P$: several or all may pass, without uniqueness or others failing. It binds only $x$ in its scope. Specifying domain, predicate and remaining parameters permits evaluation; fixing parameters does not bind them.

“There is a red token” asserts; “Find one” commands. A sealed bag may contain one without our knowledge: unknown is not false. An empty bag has no witness, even within a nonempty universe.


<span id="from-existence-and-witnesses-read-aloud"></span>

### Read aloud

There exists at least one x in D such that P of x holds.

<span id="from-bound-variables-and-scope"></span>

## Bound variables and scope

For $D=\{1,2\}$ and fixed real comparison value $y$, consider:

$$\forall x\in D\;(x<y),$$

Every member is compared with fixed $y$: true at $y=3$, false at $y=2$, where the member two fails strict comparison.


<span id="from-bound-variables-and-scope-classify-occurrences-not-letters-forever"></span>

### Classify occurrences, not letters forever

A variable **occurrence** is **bound** by its named quantifier within that quantifier’s **scope** (governed subformula); otherwise it is **free**. A same-named inner quantifier takes over locally. **Open** formulas have free occurrences; **closed** ones have none, not necessarily no letters.

Here $x$ is bound, $y$ free; the $x$ after $\forall$ declares a name, not an external input. Assigning $y=3$ evaluates this open formula without binding $y$.


<span id="from-bound-variables-and-scope-a-safe-new-name-keeps-the-roles"></span>

### A safe new name keeps the roles

A fresh label gives

$$\forall t\in D\;(t<y).$$

Rename the declaration and exactly its bound occurrences, leaving domain and free $y$ unchanged: the same inputs face the same comparison.

Using $y$ instead yields $\forall y\in D\;(y<y)$: every member less than itself, false here, unlike the original at three. This **variable capture** binds the formerly free comparison value.

Changing only the declaration loses its old occurrences. Global replacement risks other binders and free occurrences.


<span id="from-bound-variables-and-scope-parentheses-determine-the-reach"></span>

### Parentheses determine the reach

Compare

$$(\forall x\in D\;(x<3))\land(x>0)$$

with

$$\forall x\in D\;((x<3)\land(x>0)).$$

The first is a conjunction: the quantifier stops before the final free $x$. At real $x=-1$ it is false despite its true universal part; this free value need not lie in $D$.

The second quantifies both comparisons: one and two are below three and positive, so it is true. No free $x$ remains for an external assignment to affect.

In $\forall x\in D\;((x<y)\land(y<3))$, both $y$ occurrences remain free despite lying inside the scope. At $y=5/2$ it is true; at $y=3$ the second comparison fails.


<span id="from-bound-variables-and-scope-moving-a-fixed-condition-needs-a-domain-check"></span>

### Moving a fixed condition needs a domain check

Let $C$ be a proposition independent of $x$. Compare

$$\forall x\in D\;(P(x)\land C)$$

and

$$(\forall x\in D\;P(x))\land C.$$

The first requires $P$ and $C$ at every member; the second asserts $C$ separately. For empty $D$ and false $C$, only the first is vacuously true: the rewrite fails.

For nonempty $D$, the first yields every $P$ and, using one member, $C$. Conversely, separate universal $P$ and $C$ give both at each member. This proves equivalence and identifies the missing empty-domain step.

### When an inner quantifier reuses a name

For $D=\{1,2\}$, $E=\{-1\}$, $\forall x\in D\;((x>0)\land(\forall x\in E\;(x<0)))$ is true: outer inputs are positive, inner ones negative, not one input both. Rename the inner binder and its comparison to $t$; an outer renaming must leave inner occurrences untouched.

<Exercise question="For $D=\{1,2\}$, rename the bound variable of $(\forall x\in D\;(x<3))\land(x>0)$ to $t$; evaluate at free $x=-1$. Does the final occurrence change?">

This gives $(\forall t\in D\;(t<3))\land(x>0)$: only declaration and bound comparison change; final $x$ stays free. At $x=-1$, true universal part and false comparison give false conjunction.

</Exercise>

<Exercise question="Replacing $\forall x\in D\;(x\le y)$ by $\forall y\in D\;(y\le y)$ preserves truth at free $y=3$. Why is it unsafe? Separate them by assignment; does knowing a free value bind it?">

The replacement captures $y$ and is always true by reflexivity. At original $y=0$, one and two refute the original. Agreement at three loses this dependence; knowing a value permits evaluation, not syntactic binding.

</Exercise>

## Assignments and closed formulas

<span id="from-aussagen-und-aussageformen-free-occurrences-scope-and-assignments"></span>

### Free occurrences, scope, and assignments

$\mathbb Z$ means integers, $\mathbb R$ reals; $x\in\mathbb R$ states membership, not equality. “Let $x$ be real” often introduces an **arbitrary fixed** proof input; “let $x=2$” specifies one. Neither adds a written quantifier.

$$\forall x\in\mathbb R\;(x+y=y+x)$$

Both $x$ occurrences are bound; $y$ remains free despite commutativity holding at every real assignment. Universal truth across assignments does not insert a quantifier; adding $\forall y\in\mathbb R$ closes it:

$$\forall x\in\mathbb R\;\forall y\in\mathbb R\;(x+y=y+x).$$

Assigning $y=0$ supplies context without binding; substituting zero replaces occurrences and creates a **different written formula**.

$\exists x\in\mathbb R\;(x=y)$ likewise stays open in $y$, despite a witness at every assignment; existence does not assert uniqueness. In $\forall x\in\mathbb R\;(x=y)$, assigning $y=0$ leaves $y$ free but makes the claim false at $x=1$.
