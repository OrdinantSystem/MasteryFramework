## The claim

<Math tex="\forall n\in\mathbb Z:\quad 2\mid n\iff2\mid n^2" display />

“Even” means $n = 2k$ for an integer $k$. “Odd” means $n = 2k + 1$. An equivalence requires both directions; proving only one implication leaves half the work undone.

Read the claim: “For every integer $n$, two divides $n$ if and only if two divides its square.” The [biconditional foundation](../biconditionals-and-equivalent-conditions/) explains why this requires two implications. We use the ordinary integer fact that every integer is exactly one of even or odd. Thus not even means odd, and not odd means even; this dichotomy justifies the negations in the reverse proof.

## Forward: use the definition

<Math tex="n=2k\quad\Longrightarrow\quad n^2=4k^2=2(2k^2)" display />

The term in parentheses is an integer, so the square is even.

## Backward: use contraposition

<Math tex="n=2k+1\quad\Longrightarrow\quad n^2=4k^2+4k+1=2(2k^2+2k)+1" display />

An odd input has an odd square. This proves the contrapositive of “if the square is even, then the input is even”. The overall equivalence holds for negative integers and zero too; the odd-form calculation itself does not represent zero. Zero is even and has even square, so the forward argument covers it. Do not silently restrict the domain to positive numbers.

## Why use contraposition here?

Starting from $n^2 = 2k$ does not immediately write $n$ as twice an integer: taking a square root leaves the very question we need to settle. Oddness has a more useful form, $n = 2k + 1$. Squaring it produces an even part plus 1, so odd inputs cannot have even squares. This excludes the only obstruction to the reverse implication. Notice that $k$ may be negative; no positivity assumption was used.

## Practice

Use a representation supplied by the definition, then identify the integer left inside the final parentheses.

<Exercise question="Prove that the product of two odd integers is odd.">

Write $a = 2r + 1$ and $b = 2s + 1$. Their product is $4rs+2r+2s+1=2(2rs+r+s)+1$. The parenthesized quantity is an integer, giving the required odd form.

</Exercise>
