## Reading sequence convergence

A device reports nine twice, then three at every later observation. The complete rule, not a guess from three observations, tells us that all terms from the third onward equal three. Convergence allows the early exceptions. It asks whether every sufficiently late term stays as close to a fixed target as requested.


<span id="from-reading-sequence-convergence-fix-the-objects-before-reading-the-quantifiers"></span>

### Fix the objects before reading the quantifiers

Fix a real sequence $a:\mathbb N\to\mathbb R$ and a real target $L$. Here $\mathbb N=\{1,2,3,\ldots\}$. The term $a_n$ is an output, while $n$ is its index. Its distance from the target is $|a_n-L|$. The sequence and target stay fixed throughout; the formula below does not choose them afresh. Their contextual fixation is not a written quantifier binding them.

Epsilon, $\varepsilon$, is a positive real tolerance, not an index or a fixed tiny number. Capital $N$ is a positive natural threshold marking the beginning of a tail. Lowercase $n$ is any positive natural index to test. Both index comparisons and distance comparisons matter: $n\ge N$ includes the threshold; $|a_n-L|<\varepsilon$ excludes equality with the tolerance.


<span id="from-reading-sequence-convergence-the-positive-definition"></span>

### The positive definition

$$
\forall\varepsilon>0\;\exists N\in\mathbb N\;\forall n\in\mathbb N\;\bigl(n\ge N\Rightarrow |a_n-L|<\varepsilon\bigr).
$$

<Flow nodes={[{"label": "Tolerance", "tex": "\\varepsilon>0", "detail": "Accept any positive real tolerance."}, {"label": "Threshold", "tex": "N\\in\\mathbb N", "detail": "Choose one threshold using the tolerance."}, {"label": "Tail test", "tex": "n\\ge N", "detail": "Keep the threshold fixed for every test."}]} caption="A tolerance, then a threshold, then every tail index." />

This defines convergence to the specified target. It is not a theorem to prove. For particular sequences, we prove that they satisfy it. First accept any positive tolerance. Then supply a natural threshold using that tolerance and the fixed context. Finally keep the threshold fixed while checking every test index. A witness threshold may depend on the tolerance, but need not vary if the same one works.

The implication places no closeness requirement on earlier indices: its hypothesis is false there. At the threshold and afterward, every term must pass. Checking the threshold term alone is insufficient. Once a threshold works, every larger natural threshold works because its tail is contained in the already checked tail. No smallest threshold is required.

For the device and target three, choose threshold three for every positive tolerance. Every term in that tail equals three, so its distance is zero, strictly below the tolerance. This checks arbitrary tolerances and all later indices by the full rule, not by finite observations. Convergence asks for small distance, not for exact attainment of the target.

<span id="from-proving-sequence-convergence"></span>

## Proving sequence convergence

A sequence already equal to three at every position should converge to three. The proof is short, but each quantifier still has a job. Fix the sequence and target before accepting the arbitrary tolerance; do not choose a target separately for each term.

$$
\forall\varepsilon>0\;\exists N\in\mathbb N\;\forall n\in\mathbb N\;\bigl(n\ge N\Rightarrow |a_n-L|<\varepsilon\bigr).
$$


<span id="from-proving-sequence-convergence-the-full-constant-sequence-proof"></span>

### The full constant-sequence proof

Let $a_n=3$ for every $n\in\mathbb N$ and let $L=3$. Take any positive real $\varepsilon$. Choose $N=1$, which is a positive natural number. Now take any natural $n\ge N$. The sequence rule gives $a_n=3$, hence:

$$
|a_n-3|=|3-3|=|0|=0<\varepsilon.
$$

The first equality uses the sequence rule; the next uses subtraction of equal reals; the next uses the absolute value at zero; the final strict inequality uses positivity of the arbitrary tolerance. Every tested index passes. The tolerance was arbitrary, so the definition holds. Neither natural-number unboundedness nor the triangle inequality was used.


<span id="from-proving-sequence-convergence-choice-dependence-and-proof-dependence"></span>

### Choice dependence and proof dependence

Choice dependence asks which information $N$ may use: the tolerance and fixed context, but not the later universal $n$. Proof dependence asks which facts justify the distance estimate. Correct order cannot repair false arithmetic, and correct arithmetic cannot repair a late witness choice. The constant threshold may ignore the tolerance because it works for all of them.

For first two terms nine and every later term three, choose $N=3$ for every positive tolerance. Any $n\ge3$ has term three and distance zero. Early exceptions are outside the demanded tail. If $N$ works and $M\ge N$ is natural, every $n\ge M$ also satisfies $n\ge N$, so $M$ works too. Minimality is unnecessary.

<Note title="A late threshold changes the claim">

Choosing $N=n+1$ after seeing each test index makes that index earlier than its new threshold. The implication then holds vacuously for that index, but no single threshold has been supplied for all later indices. The error is not that the threshold is large; it is that it changes with a forbidden later input.

</Note>

<span id="from-reciprocal-sequence-convergence"></span>

## Reciprocal sequence convergence

The terms one, one half, one third and so on suggest approaching zero without ever reaching it. A complete proof needs more than a picture: it must supply a natural threshold for every positive real tolerance and justify every later estimate.

<Note title="Optional extension: additional accepted background">

This page is optional. The main route to [negating convergence](../negating-sequence-convergence/#from-negating-sequence-convergence) does not depend on it. We accept, without proving here, the Archimedean property: every real bound is exceeded by some positive natural number. We also use positive reciprocal order: increasing a positive denominator decreases or preserves its reciprocal. These are mathematical prerequisites for this example, not consequences of writing quantifiers.

</Note>


<span id="from-reciprocal-sequence-convergence-find-a-threshold-then-prove-its-tail-works"></span>

### Find a threshold, then prove its tail works

$$
a_n=\frac1n\quad(n\in\mathbb N),\qquad L=0.
$$

All indices are positive, so every term is well-defined and positive. Take any real $\varepsilon>0$. Its reciprocal $1/\varepsilon$ is real. The accepted Archimedean property gives a positive natural $N>1/\varepsilon$. Fix that threshold before testing indices. For any natural $n\ge N$:

$$
|a_n-0|=|1/n|=1/n\le1/N<\varepsilon.
$$

The first equality uses the sequence rule and subtracting zero. Positivity of $1/n$ removes the absolute value. Positive reciprocal order gives $1/n\le1/N$ from $n\ge N$. The strict choice $N>1/\varepsilon$ gives $1/N<\varepsilon$: multiplying the first inequality by positive $\varepsilon$ gives $N\varepsilon>1$, then dividing by positive $N$ gives the result. Hence every term in the tail meets the tolerance. Since the tolerance was arbitrary, the sequence converges to zero.

Every term remains positive, so no term equals the limit. Convergence requires small distance in every sufficiently late tail, not equality at some position. This proof uses its two extra facts to cover infinitely many tolerances and indices; displaying a long finite list would not do that.


<span id="from-reciprocal-sequence-convergence-a-strict-boundary-check"></span>

### A strict boundary check

At $\varepsilon=1/10$, $N=11$ works since $1/11<1/10$ and later reciprocals are no larger. $N=10$ fails at $n=10$, where the distance equals the tolerance. These rational checks illustrate strictness; the arbitrary-tolerance proof above is the general argument. Any larger natural threshold than a successful one also works.
