## Reading mathematical structure

“Find a real number less than two” is an instruction. “Every real number is less than two” makes a claim, and it is false. The first task in reading mathematics is to identify what the sentence does. A long string of symbols does not settle that question.


<span id="from-reading-mathematical-structure-a-reading-procedure"></span>

### A reading procedure

Identify the sentence’s job: definition, assertion, local assumption, question or instruction. Then check domains and definedness (no real division by zero), free and bound occurrences, and any values fixed by context. Preserve quantifier order and scope: $\forall$ means every, $\exists$ at least one, not exactly one. Parse from the outermost connective inward, keeping each whole input together. In a conditional, identify the whole hypothesis and conclusion; do not invent a hypothesis for a simple equality. Parsing, evaluation and proof remain separate.

Addition in $x+y=3$ combines numbers inside one condition, not two propositions. At the propositional level we may treat a whole quantified statement as one input letter, but that does not make it an atomic formula in first-order syntax.


<span id="from-reading-mathematical-structure-a-universal-claim-with-a-conditional-inside"></span>

### A universal claim with a conditional inside

“Any real number strictly between zero and one is less than two” becomes

$$\forall x\in\mathbb R\;(((0<x)\land(x<1))\Rightarrow(x<2)).$$

“For every real x, if zero is strictly less than x and x is strictly less than one, then x is strictly less than two.”

The quantifier governs the entire conditional and binds every occurrence of $x$. The formula is closed. Inside its scope the main connective is $\Rightarrow$, also written $\to$. Its left input is the entire conjunction; its right input is $x<2$. The symbol $\land$ means “and,” not a second outer connective.

Keep both $0<x$ and $x<1$ in the translation. A later proof may not need the lower bound, but removing it while translating changes the stated hypothesis. Nor does the conditional assert that every real number lies between zero and one.

At $x=1/2$, both hypothesis and conclusion hold. At $x=3$, the hypothesis fails, so three is not a counterexample, even though the conclusion fails too. A counterexample to a universal conditional must satisfy its **entire hypothesis** and fail its conclusion.

For a proof, take any real $x$ satisfying the hypothesis. From $x<1$ and $1<2$, transitivity of the usual real order gives $x<2$. This arbitrary-input reasoning proves the claim. The translation and numerical checks alone did not. Classical semantics and ordinary real order are the accepted foundations here.


<span id="from-reading-mathematical-structure-practice-in-both-directions"></span>

### Practice in both directions

<Exercise question="Parse $x=0\lor((x>0)\land(x<1))$. Give the outer connective, its two whole inputs, and the free variables. Then translate ‘x equals zero and it is not the case that y equals zero’.">

The outer connective is $\lor$. Its inputs are $x=0$ and the whole conjunction $(x>0)\land(x<1)$. All occurrences of $x$ remain free unless context adds a quantifier. The second translation is $(x=0)\land\neg(y=0)$; only the second equality is negated, and both variables remain free as written.

</Exercise>

Translate “Every real number greater than one is greater than zero.” Identify the hypothesis and prove the claim without a list of successful examples. Does it claim that every real is positive?

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>

“For every number with a property” contains a conditional. Keep the domain outside that conditional.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Reference answer</summary>

$\forall x\in\mathbb R\;((x>1)\Rightarrow(x>0))$. The hypothesis is $x>1$; the conclusion is $x>0$. For arbitrary real $x$ above one, $1>0$ and transitivity give $x>0$. The quantifier binds every occurrence of $x$. The claim does not assert positivity of all reals: zero fails the hypothesis and is not a counterexample.

</details>

When checking a translation, look for changes from “if” to “and,” “some” to “every,” or strict to non-strict bounds. If the original wording is genuinely ambiguous, state the ambiguity. ## Quantifier order

Ana can use only red; Ben can use only blue. These are all the people, keys and permissions. Each person has a key, but no key works for everyone. The same objects answer two different questions.

| Person | Red | Blue |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ana | yes | no |
| Ben | no | yes |

Let $P$ be people, $K$ keys and $H(p,k)$ mean that person $p$ can use key $k$. Read the whole remainder as a bracket after each quantifier.

$$
\forall p\in P\;\exists k\in K\;H(p,k).
$$

For each person, a usable key exists. Red witnesses Ana, blue witnesses Ben. The choices may differ, but need not.

$$
\exists k\in K\;\forall p\in P\;H(p,k).
$$

Here choose a key for which the whole every-person claim holds. Red fails for Ben and blue fails for Ana; all candidates are excluded. A nested quantifier lies within another quantifier’s scope. Two separately quantified statements joined by “and” need not be nested. In these formulas both variables are bound and the first scope contains the second quantifier. This is logical order, not physical time or a promised algorithm.


<span id="from-quantifier-order-the-same-distinction-over-the-reals"></span>

### The same distinction over the reals

$$
\forall x\in\mathbb R\;\exists y\in\mathbb R\;(y>x).
$$

True: for arbitrary real $x$, choose $y=x+1$. Closure of addition gives a real output, and positivity of one gives $y>x$. These reasons cover every input, not just tested examples.

$$
\exists y\in\mathbb R\;\forall x\in\mathbb R\;(y>x).
$$

False: for any proposed $y$, test the allowed value $x=y$. It would require $y>y$. The test defeats every proposed witness. The recipe $y=x+1$ cannot prove this order because it changes the supposedly fixed witness with the later input.


<span id="from-quantifier-order-safe-swaps-have-conditions"></span>

### Safe swaps have conditions

For fixed independent domains $D,E$, adjacent universal quantifiers commute: either order tests every ordered pair. To prove either direction, take any pair and apply the original two requirements to its entries. If either domain is empty, both orders are true. Adjacent existential quantifiers also commute: a successful pair can be presented in either order. If either domain is empty, both orders are false. Keep $R(x,y)$ unchanged; swapping binders does not swap relation arguments. A domain $E(x)$ cannot simply be moved before $x$ is introduced.

<span id="from-witness-dependence"></span>

## Witness dependence

The larger-real example shows why $y=x+1$ needs the input first. With three quantifiers, track exactly which inputs each witness may use.


<span id="from-witness-dependence-keep-an-information-ledger"></span>

### Keep an information ledger

At a witness choice, list fixed parameters, earlier arbitrary inputs in whose scope it lies and earlier chosen witnesses. These are available. Later universally tested inputs cannot change an earlier choice. Dependence is permitted, not required: choosing positive one may ignore an earlier input when only positivity is requested. An existential assertion does not promise a computable method.

$$
\forall x\in\mathbb R\;\forall y\in\mathbb R\;\exists z\in\mathbb R\;(z=x+y).
$$

Both $x$ and $y$ are available. Choose $z=x+y$; closure of real addition supplies membership and the chosen equality supplies the predicate. The argument applies to any real pair.

$$
\forall x\in\mathbb R\;\exists z\in\mathbb R\;\forall y\in\mathbb R\;(z=x+y).
$$

Now $z$ may use $x$, but must remain fixed for every later $y$. At $y=0$ the condition forces $z=x$; at $y=1$ it forces $z=x+1$. The same value cannot satisfy both. This defeats every candidate using two incompatible requirements, not an inference from a sample.

A later envelope choice may use a previously selected key: earlier existential choices can coordinate. Binding is a different matter. In a formula with fixed real $c$, the recipe $y=x+c$ may use $c$, although its occurrences remain syntactically free if no binder for $c$ is written. The context fixes its value. A bound witness may depend on an earlier input without becoming free.

Legal information is not enough for success. The constant $y=1$ uses no forbidden input but fails $y>x$ at $x=1$. The rule $y=x/2$ uses only the earlier input, yet fails integer membership when $x=1$. Always check information, membership and the whole predicate separately.

## Separate and shared witnesses

Add a green key both may use: now both requirements hold. Add two master keys: either can witness the shared claim. A shared witness is held fixed, not necessarily unique.

$$
\underbrace{\exists k\in K\;\forall p\in P\;H(p,k)}_{\text{S}}\ \Rightarrow\ \underbrace{\forall p\in P\;\exists k\in K\;H(p,k)}_{\text{T}}.
$$

Keep the people, keys and permissions from the red–blue model. To prove the implication, assume S and fix a key it supplies. Take any allowed person. The key works for that person because it works for all. Reuse it as that person’s witness. No distinctness of choices is required. Thus S implies T. The complete red–blue model refutes the converse.

Stronger means that shared success implies separate success. It does not mean opposite truth values in every situation. With green both are true; if someone has no usable key both are false. A finite complete permission table can establish these finite claims; a sample from an infinite domain cannot settle every case.

<span id="from-separate-and-shared-witnesses-all-empty-domain-cases"></span>

### All empty-domain cases

| People | Keys | Separate T | Shared S |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| empty | nonempty | true | true |
| nonempty | empty | false | false |
| empty | empty | true | false |

With no people, the universal-first form has no failing person. The shared form still needs a key: any existing key works vacuously, but if no key exists the form is false. With people but no keys, each person lacks a witness. The implication S to T remains valid in every case. A true premise supplies a key to reuse; a false premise makes the implication true.

For real comparisons, [quantifier order](../quantifier-order/#from-quantifier-order) proves separate $y>x$ true and shared $y>x$ false. For the non-strict variant $y\ge x$, separate success follows even with $y=x$. Shared success still fails, but testing $x=y$ no longer defeats it: test $x=y+1$ instead.

## Translating quantified statements

Transfer the key model to books: translate “Every student has read a book” and “There is a book every student has read”. The distinction is unchanged; the domains and relation are new.

Let $S$ be students, $B$ books and $R(s,b)$ mean that $s$ has read $b$. Keep that relation unchanged; do not silently switch from reading to ownership.

$$
\forall s\in S\;\exists b\in B\;R(s,b).
$$

$$
\exists b\in B\;\forall s\in S\;R(s,b).
$$

Read back the first as “for each student, a book they have read”; the second as “a book read by every student”. Neither requires exactly one book. The first does not assert that students exist. With no students it is true; the second still requires an actual book.


<span id="from-translating-quantified-statements-write-then-read-back"></span>

### Write, then read back

Specify each domain and the final relation. Decide which requirement governs the rest, then write the binders and their scopes. Check every variable: bound here or fixed in context? Finally read the formula back and test a small situation where a tempting alternative differs. Translation and proof are separate tasks. Preserve a false assertion faithfully instead of changing its order to make it true.

$$
\forall x\in\mathbb R\;\forall y\in\mathbb R\;\exists z\in\mathbb R\;(z=x+y).
$$

This translates “Every pair of reals has a real sum”. Both inputs precede the witness. Its truth additionally uses closure of real addition; the quantifier symbols alone do not prove that arithmetic fact.


<span id="from-translating-quantified-statements-a-conditional-limits-the-requirement"></span>

### A conditional limits the requirement

$$
\forall s\in S\;\bigl(A(s)\Rightarrow\exists b\in B\;R(s,b)\bigr).
$$

Here $A(s)$ means attending the seminar. Only attending students must supply a read book. Moving $\exists b$ outside the conditional can demand a book for nonattenders too. Take one nonattending student and no books: the displayed conditional is true, but $\forall s\in S\;\exists b\in B\;(A(s)\Rightarrow R(s,b))$ is false. Parentheses and empty domains expose the difference. Restricting $S$ explicitly to attenders is another faithful translation.
