## The language and structure of proof

<span id="from-beweissprache-und-methoden-choose-a-method"></span>

### Choose a method

- Direct proof: start from the hypotheses and derive the conclusion.
- Contraposition: prove that a false conclusion forces a false premise.
- Contradiction: assume the negation of the goal and derive an impossibility.
- Counterexample: refute a universal claim with one permitted case.
- Induction: prove a starting case and a step from $n$ to $n + 1$; this is a method for a later lesson, not a substitute for either step.


<span id="from-beweissprache-und-methoden-a-small-workflow"></span>

### A small workflow

State the claim and domain, unpack definitions, choose a method and justify the steps and conclusion. For $\exists n \in  \mathbb{N} : n^2 = 9$, witness $n = 3$ suffices; $n = 1$ refutes $\forall n \in  \mathbb{N} : n^2 > n$. Never justify a step with the claim being proved: that is circular.


<span id="from-beweissprache-und-methoden-arbitrary-does-not-mean-a-representative-sample"></span>

### Arbitrary does not mean a representative sample

Fix an arbitrary real $x$. Adding $x$ to $2>0$ gives $x+2>x+0=x$: addition preserves order and zero changes nothing. This proves $\forall x\in\mathbb R\;(x+2>x)$ without restricting $x$.

For a universal conditional, assume the whole hypothesis and derive the conclusion. False hypotheses already satisfy the conditional; no eligible input need exist. An empty restricted collection has no failure. Temporary membership assumptions can be discharged without asserting existence. See [domains](../universal-statements-and-domains/#from-universal-statements-and-domains) and [counterexamples](../beweissprache-und-methoden/#from-counterexamples).

<Exercise question="An all-real proof assumes positivity, then checks seven. What does this establish? How should a necessary positivity assumption be handled?">

The check covers only seven; a general positive-input argument covers at most positive reals. Prove the original claim without positivity or add justified zero and negative cases. Alternatively prove an explicitly restricted theorem, not the original. “Arbitrary” does not cancel restrictions.

</Exercise>


<span id="from-beweissprache-und-methoden-from-a-definition-to-a-proof"></span>

### From a definition to a proof

For even integers, choose separate integer witnesses $a = 2r$, $b = 2s$. Then $a + b = 2(r+s)$ is even because $r+s$ is an integer. Reusing $r$ would wrongly assume equal inputs.

<span id="from-counterexamples"></span>

## Counterexamples

Two and minus two have positive squares, but $0^2=0$ refutes “every real square is strictly positive,” regardless of successful nonzero examples.


<span id="from-counterexamples-why-one-failure-is-enough"></span>

### Why one failure is enough

A **counterexample** to $\forall x\in D\;P(x)$ is a member of $D$ with a meaningful, false predicate. One verified failure contradicts “every”; it does not mean every member fails.

For a universal conditional, check domain membership, the **whole** true hypothesis and false conclusion at the **same object**, since

$$\neg(P\Rightarrow Q)\equiv(P\land\neg Q).$$

This [conditional negation](../implikation-und-kontraposition/#from-implikation-und-kontraposition) is not the inverse $\neg P\Rightarrow\neg Q$.


<span id="from-counterexamples-find-the-full-failure-region"></span>

### Find the full failure region

Consider

$$\forall x\in\mathbb R\;((x>2)\Rightarrow(x>5)).$$

Failure requires $x>2$ and $x\le5$: exactly $2<x\le5$.

| Candidate | Hypothesis $x>2$ | Conclusion $x>5$ | Counterexample? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $0$ | False | False | No: false hypothesis |
| $2$ | False | False | No: strict lower boundary |
| $3$ | True | False | Yes |
| $5$ | True | False | Yes: equality fails the conclusion |
| $7$ | True | True | No: conclusion holds |

Two is excluded; five included. A false conclusion alone cannot refute the claim, nor can combining seven’s true hypothesis with zero’s false conclusion. Three refutes this rule, not the opposite “above five implies above two.”

The marked interval contains exactly the counterexamples to this rule. Test both endpoints: two fails the hypothesis, while five satisfies the hypothesis and fails the strict conclusion.

<span id="from-counterexamples-do-not-drop-a-compound-requirement"></span>

### Do not drop a compound requirement

For real $x$, $((x>0)\land(x<2))\Rightarrow(x^2>1)$ fails at one: both hypotheses hold but $1^2=1$. Zero fails positivity; three fails the upper bound. Neither is a counterexample.

$0<x<1\Rightarrow x<1/2$ fails at $3/4$ and $1/2$; equality defeats the strict conclusion. Two fails the upper hypothesis.


<span id="from-counterexamples-search-verification-and-repair"></span>

### Search, verification, and repair

Try permitted zeros, small integers, negatives or endpoints, then verify. Finite unsuccessful searches do not prove infinite-domain claims. Undefined predicates require a repaired domain or interpretation, not refutation. Keep free parameters fixed throughout.

Zero refutes strict positivity but proves no replacement. For **nonzero** reals, positive times positive and negative times negative are positive by the sign rules; these cases exhaust the domain. Including zero proves nonnegativity of all real squares.

<Exercise question="Test $-1/2$ against ‘every integer has square at least one’, then refute the claim correctly. Does an invalid candidate prove it true?">

$-1/2$ is outside the integer domain. Zero is an integer with $0^2=0<1$, refuting the claim. An invalid candidate proves neither truth nor falsity.

</Exercise>

<Exercise question="Invent a universal conditional with two hypothesis requirements, a counterexample and a tempting invalid candidate. Check each requirement. Why does not-all differ from none?">

For real $x$, claim $1<x<4\Rightarrow x>2$. Two meets both hypotheses but fails the conclusion. Zero fails the lower bound; five the upper. Three satisfies both hypothesis and conclusion, so not all eligible instances fail.

</Exercise>

## Equivalence and parity

<span id="from-aequivalenz-und-paritaet-the-claim"></span>

### The claim

<Math tex="\forall n\in\mathbb Z:\quad 2\mid n\iff2\mid n^2" display />

For integer $k$, even means $n = 2k$; odd means $n = 2k + 1$. The [equivalence](../biconditionals-and-equivalent-conditions/#from-biconditionals-and-equivalent-conditions) needs both implications.

We use the integer fact that every integer is exactly one of even or odd. Thus not even means odd and vice versa, justifying contraposition below.


<span id="from-aequivalenz-und-paritaet-forward-use-the-definition"></span>

### Forward: use the definition

<Math tex="n=2k\quad\Longrightarrow\quad n^2=4k^2=2(2k^2)" display />

The term in parentheses is an integer, so the square is even.


<span id="from-aequivalenz-und-paritaet-backward-use-contraposition"></span>

### Backward: use contraposition

<Math tex="n=2k+1\quad\Longrightarrow\quad n^2=4k^2+4k+1=2(2k^2+2k)+1" display />

Odd inputs have odd squares: the contrapositive of “even square implies even input.” Negative integers are included. Zero is not represented by the odd form; it is even with even square, covered by the forward proof.


<span id="from-aequivalenz-und-paritaet-why-use-contraposition-here"></span>

### Why use contraposition here?

From $n^2=2k$, taking a square root does not show $n$ is twice an integer. Instead $n=2k+1$ squares to an even part plus one, excluding odd inputs. The witness $k$ may be negative.


<span id="from-aequivalenz-und-paritaet-practice"></span>

### Practice

Use the definition and identify the integer in the final parentheses.

<Exercise question="Prove that the product of two odd integers is odd.">

Write $a = 2r + 1$ and $b = 2s + 1$. Their product is $4rs+2r+2s+1=2(2rs+r+s)+1$. The parenthesized quantity is an integer, giving the required odd form.

</Exercise>
