## Logical connectives

<span id="from-logische-verknuepfungen-one-card-two-properties"></span>

### One card, two properties

Fix one integer card. $P$ claims positivity (greater than zero); $Q$ claims evenness (integer quotient on division by two). Letters name claims, not numbers. Ordinary arithmetic and order give:

| Integer | $P$ | $Q$ | $P\land Q$ | $P\lor Q$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 6 | 1 | 1 | 1 | 1 |
| 9 | 1 | 0 | 0 | 1 |
| -2 | 0 | 1 | 0 | 1 |
| -3 | 0 | 0 | 0 | 0 |
| 0 | 0 | 1 | 0 | 1 |

Six satisfies both; nine only positivity; negative two only evenness; negative three neither. Zero is even since $0/2=0$, not positive. A false component means false conjunction, not meaninglessness.

Prove **both** conjuncts: $6>0$ and integer $6/2=3$. One failure refutes: twelve is positive but not below ten. A true conjunction yields each component; a false one does not say which failed.

Prove **one** disjunct, without requiring the other false; refutation needs **both** false. Negative three fails both. Nonpositivity alone leaves evenness possible, as negative two shows.


<span id="from-logische-verknuepfungen-inclusive-or-and-exactly-one"></span>

### Inclusive or and exactly one

Inclusive $P\lor Q$ allows overlap; **exactly one** does not. Only $11$ differs: inclusive gives $1$, exclusive $0$. Both give $1,1,0$ on $10,01,00$.

A menu may restrict “soup or salad” to one choice; context, not “either” alone, establishes exclusivity. Strict negativity and positivity cannot overlap by real order, not by a different OR rule. Abstract tables still include the both-true assignment.

“Simultaneous” concerns context, not necessarily one instant: “The door was open at noon and closed at one” may be true. AND implies neither time order nor causation. “Positive **but** odd” is conjunction with emphasis, not a new rule.


<span id="from-logische-verknuepfungen-grouping-and-atomic-inputs"></span>

### Grouping and atomic inputs

At this propositional level, an **atomic** claim is treated as one unsplit unit; a **compound** uses connectives on one or more claims.

$x+1=3$ is one condition: addition combines numbers, not claims. A quantified statement can serve as one input without being first-order atomic. $(x>0)\land(x<10)$ leaves $x$ free; assign the same value to both parts.

Let $P$ mean a ticket, $Q$ a reservation, $R$ a guest pass:

$$P\land(Q\lor R)\qquad (P\land Q)\lor R.$$

The first requires a ticket plus reservation or pass; the second accepts a pass alone. $P=0,Q=0,R=1$ gives false versus true, proving different general meanings.

Pure AND or inclusive OR chains regroup, as justified below; mixed connectives require checking.

<details>
<summary>Notation readings</summary>

$P\land Q$: “P and Q”; $P\lor Q$: “P or Q, or both”; $P\land(Q\lor R)$: “P and, in addition, at least one of Q or R”. Some books write $\to$ for $\Rightarrow$ and $\leftrightarrow$ for $\Leftrightarrow$.

</details>


<span id="from-logische-verknuepfungen-practice-with-reasons"></span>

### Practice with reasons

Cards show nine and negative two. Does that prove a fixed hidden card positive and even? Identify two gaps.

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>

Which card was checked? Did one integer pass both?

</details>

<details>
<summary>Reference answer</summary>

Neither example concerns the hidden card; positivity and evenness were also checked on different integers. Both claims must concern that same fixed card.

</details>

<Exercise question="A card qualifies by a star or by circle plus blue border. Write the S, C, B criterion; test star-only and circle-only cards.">

$S\lor(C\land B)$. Star alone satisfies $S$, independently of the other route. Circle alone lacks both star and required border, so fails. Keep the inner checklist grouped.

</Exercise>

<Exercise question="Given true $P\lor Q$, is P determined? What if Q is false? Given only false $P\land Q$, must Q be false?">

Initially $P$ may be either value: $10$ and $01$ qualify. If $Q=0$, truth requires $P=1$. False conjunction does not force false $Q$: $01$ refutes that. True conjunction does force every component, including $Q$, true.

</Exercise>

<span id="from-simultaneous-conditions"></span>

## Several simultaneous conditions

Locally define an **admissible** integer as positive, below ten, and even. This is not standard terminology. Six passes; nine fails evenness; ten fails strictness. Two successes cannot compensate for one failure.


<span id="from-simultaneous-conditions-read-one-structured-criterion"></span>

### Read one structured criterion

All tests concern **one integer**; a positive noninteger below ten already fails the domain.

With $A$ positive, $B$ below ten, $C$ even, both

$$(A\land B)\land C\qquad\text{and}\qquad A\land(B\land C)$$

require all three: the first requires $A,B$ plus $C$; the second $B,C$ plus $A$. This proves associativity semantically for arbitrary claims, not by four integer checks. Either inclusive-OR grouping requires at least one true input. Mixed connectives still need checking.

$A\land(B\land C)$ reads “A, and both B and C.” Conjunction binds no variable.

Fix domain and object; separate conditions; distinguish all from at least one; preserve inner alternatives; distinguish definition from claim. Commas do not choose connectives.


<span id="from-simultaneous-conditions-establish-a-label-then-unpack-it"></span>

### Establish a label, then unpack it

Iff supplies both directions: $6>0$, $6<10$, integer $6/2=3$ prove admissibility; admissibility recovers **each** property, not a unique value.

Exactly $2,4,6,8$ qualify: bounds leave integers $1$–$9$, whose even members are those four. Exhaustion, not four successes alone, proves completeness. Definition fixes meaning; candidate reasoning proves the list.


<span id="from-simultaneous-conditions-a-sufficient-rule-is-different"></span>

### A sufficient rule is different

Suppose the ordinary rule “An integer receives a blue mark if positive, below ten, and even” is **not** a full definition. The checklist guarantees marking, without forbidding extra marks.

Marking every integer obeys it, yet marked nine is odd: a mark does not imply evenness. Replacing “if” by **iff** would license unpacking.

Defining contexts sometimes intend iff through abbreviated “if”; this does not make them generally equivalent. Write “means” or “iff” for both directions; flag ambiguity rather than add a converse.


<span id="from-simultaneous-conditions-practice-with-alternatives-and-failures"></span>

### Practice with alternatives and failures

Require an integer to be positive and (even **or equal to three**). Write the criterion; test $3,6,-2,9$.

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>

Two outer requirements; the second has two routes.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Reference answer</summary>

$P\land(E\lor T)$, with positivity $P$, evenness $E$, equality to three $T$. Three passes via $P,T$; six via $P,E$. Negative two fails $P$; nine fails both inner alternatives. The grouped alternative does not demand all three.

</details>

<Exercise question="An integer qualifies if zero, or if positive and below ten. Write the criterion using A, B, C in that order; test zero and ten.">

$A\lor(B\land C)$. Zero passes $A$ without positivity. Ten fails $A,C$, hence both routes. Requiring zero positive changes the outer connective.

</Exercise>

<Exercise question="Unpack original admissibility. Must the integer be six? Describe nonadmissibility; test zero, nine and ten at its boundaries.">

It is positive, below ten and even, but may be two instead of six. Nonadmissibility is $n\le0$ **or** $n\ge10$ **or** not even, still with $n\in\mathbb Z$. Zero fails only positivity, nine only evenness, ten only strictness; not all tests must fail. See [combined negation](../logical-negation/#from-negating-combined-conditions) for proof.

</Exercise>

Invent a three-condition definition and a one-failure near-miss. Replace iff by ordinary one-way if; identify which inference from the label is lost.
