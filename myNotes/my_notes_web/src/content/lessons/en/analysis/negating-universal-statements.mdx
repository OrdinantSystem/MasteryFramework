## Negating universal statements

A tray has a red card and a blue card. “Every card is red” is false: the blue card is enough to refute it. “Every card is not red” is also false because a red card remains. Two sentences false together cannot be exact negations.


<span id="from-negating-universal-statements-not-every-means-some-failure"></span>

### Not every means some failure

$$
\neg(\forall x\in D\;P(x))\ \Longleftrightarrow\ \exists x\in D\;\neg P(x).
$$

Fix the domain $D$, the predicate $P$ and any other parameters. In classical two-valued logic, an exact negation has the opposite truth value in every meaningful situation. The equivalence says that not every allowed object passes exactly when at least one allowed object fails. It permits several failures and also permits successes to remain.

For one direction, a failing member makes the universal false because that member is included. Conversely, if there were no failing member, every member would pass and the universal would be true. So a false universal has a failing member. This reasons from meanings, covers infinite domains and does not promise an algorithm to locate the failure. A finite model illustrates the rule but does not establish the general theorem.


<span id="from-negating-universal-statements-keep-the-domain-and-negate-the-whole-predicate"></span>

### Keep the domain and negate the whole predicate

$$
\neg(\forall x\in\mathbb Z\;(x>0))\ \Longleftrightarrow\ \exists x\in\mathbb Z\;(x\le0).
$$

Zero witnesses the denial: it is an integer and is not positive. A negative half fails the original predicate but is outside the integer domain. A positive integer is allowed but does not fail. If the domain instead were positive integers, the original would be true and its correct negation false. Writing a correct negation does not make it true.

For “red and numbered”, failure means not red or not numbered, with inclusive or. One red unnumbered card is enough. On an empty domain every-object claims are true and their existential negations false. Empty restricted domains are allowed even when the wider universe is nonempty. A universal claim supplies no object by itself.

## Negating existential statements

A box contains red and blue cards. Taking out a blue card does not refute “There is a red card”. The red card still witnesses it. “Some card is not red” and “Some card is red” can both be true, so they cannot be exact negations.

$$
\neg(\exists x\in D\;P(x))\ \Longleftrightarrow\ \forall x\in D\;\neg P(x).
$$

Keep $D$, $P$ and other parameters fixed. If no allowed object satisfies $P$, take any allowed candidate: it cannot satisfy $P$, so it satisfies $\neg P$. Since the candidate was arbitrary, all fail. Conversely, if $P$ is false for every allowed object, no candidate can be a witness. These two directions establish the existential dual law in classical logic, including infinite domains.

A complete finite inspection can establish nonexistence. On an infinite domain a hundred failures do not cover every candidate; a general arbitrary-candidate argument can. This differs from refuting a universal, where one failure suffices. Negating ordinary existence rules out all witnesses, not merely exactly one: two successful witnesses still make ordinary existence true.


<span id="from-negating-existential-statements-the-restriction-stays-positive"></span>

### The restriction stays positive

$$
\neg(\exists x>0\;(x<0))\ \Longleftrightarrow\ \forall x>0\;(x\ge0).
$$

The shorthand $x>0$ beside either quantifier restricts the domain to positive reals. The comparison inside parentheses is the predicate. Negation changes the claim about that same domain, not the domain to nonpositive reals. The original is false because no positive real is negative; its denial is true because any positive real is nonnegative. Zero is not an allowed candidate.

“Some integer has square zero” is true with witness zero; its denial, every integer has nonzero square, is false at that same integer. “Some real has negative square” is false using the explicitly assumed arithmetic fact that every real square is nonnegative. Logic alone does not supply that arithmetic fact. On an empty restricted domain existence is false and its universal denial true: no object contradicts it, but no object is supplied.

## Negating quantified inequalities

A promise says a reading lies strictly within one unit of a target. A reading exactly one unit away breaks it. Saying “more than one unit away” would miss that boundary failure. Before working through the quantifiers, know which side includes equality.


<span id="from-negating-quantified-inequalities-real-order-partitions-every-case"></span>

### Real order partitions every case

For real $x,c$, exactly one of below, equal and above holds. Negation keeps precisely the cases excluded by the original comparison. Removing below leaves equal or above; removing below-or-equal leaves only above. Exchanging above and below gives the other two rules. This uses the ordinary total order of the reals, not quantifier syntax alone.

$$
\begin{aligned}\neg(x<c)&\Longleftrightarrow x\ge c,\\\neg(x\le c)&\Longleftrightarrow x>c,\\\neg(x>c)&\Longleftrightarrow x\le c,\\\neg(x\ge c)&\Longleftrightarrow x<c.\end{aligned}
$$

At equality exactly one of the original comparison and its negation is true. Negating $x=c$ gives $x\ne c$, not one chosen direction; negating $x\ne c$ gives equality. The [order-comparison lesson](../logical-negation/#from-negating-order-comparisons) develops these elementary boundaries separately.


<span id="from-negating-quantified-inequalities-a-quantified-boundary-example"></span>

### A quantified boundary example

$$
\neg(\forall x>0\;(x<1))\ \Longleftrightarrow\ \exists x>0\;(x\ge1).
$$

Every positive real being below one is false. One is an allowed counterexample and lies exactly on the boundary. Zero is not allowed. We changed every to some, retained positive reals and complemented the inner comparison. A second true example such as two is unnecessary; one already proves the negation.


<span id="from-negating-quantified-inequalities-when-the-predicate-is-conditional"></span>

### When the predicate is conditional

$$
\neg(A\Rightarrow B)\ \Longleftrightarrow\ A\land\neg B.
$$

| $A$ | $B$ | $A\Rightarrow B$ | $\neg(A\Rightarrow B)$ | $A\land\neg B$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| true | true | true | false | false |
| true | false | false | true | true |
| false | true | true | false | false |
| false | false | true | false | false |

The last two columns agree in all four possible cases, proving the equivalence. A false hypothesis never refutes the implication. In the sequence application, the hypothesis will say that an index lies in a proposed tail. Failure must occur inside that tail and violate the distance conclusion. These are independent checks on the same index.

## Quantifiers and negation

<span id="from-quantoren-und-negation-a-witness-versus-every-case"></span>

### A witness versus every case

This page is a compact reference. For a first explanation, begin with [universal statements and their domains](../universal-statements-and-domains/#from-universal-statements-and-domains) and [existence and witnesses](../universal-statements-and-domains/#from-existence-and-witnesses). Then work through [bound variables and scope](../universal-statements-and-domains/#from-bound-variables-and-scope), [quantifier order](../quantifier-order/#from-quantifier-order) and [negating universal claims](../negating-universal-statements/#from-negating-universal-statements). Those lessons develop the examples and proofs behind the formulas collected here.

Existence needs one witness. A universal claim covers every element of its stated domain; one counterexample refutes it. $\exists!$ means exactly one, combining existence and uniqueness.

<Math tex="\exists x\in\mathbb N:x+3=10\quad\text{(}x=7\text{)}\qquad\neg\bigl(\forall x\in\mathbb N:x+3=10\bigr)" display />

The left-hand claim is true because $7$ belongs to the domain and satisfies the equation. The universal claim inside the negation is false: for example, $x=1$ gives $1+3=4$, not $10$. Thus both displayed claims are true, but for different reasons. One successful input establishes existence; a different, unsuccessful input refutes universality.


<span id="from-quantoren-und-negation-push-the-negation-inward"></span>

### Push the negation inward

<Math tex="\neg(\forall x\in D:P(x))\iff\exists x\in D:\neg P(x)" display />

<Math tex="\neg(\exists x\in D:P(x))\iff\forall x\in D:\neg P(x)" display />

Negation changes the quantifier and the predicate, while keeping the domain. With nested quantifiers, change each one in order. Their order matters: one common $y$ for every $x$ is stronger than a possibly different $y$ for each $x$.

<Math tex="\neg(\forall x\,\exists y:P(x,y))\iff\exists x\,\forall y:\neg P(x,y)" display />


<span id="from-quantoren-und-negation-the-boundary-case-matters"></span>

### The boundary case matters

The claim $n^2 > n$ for every natural number fails at $n = 1$. With $\mathbb{N}$ starting at 1, the correct universal statement is $n^2 \ge  n$; strict inequality holds for $n > 1$.


<span id="from-quantoren-und-negation-order-of-quantifiers"></span>

### Order of quantifiers

Over $\mathbb{N}$, “for every $x$ there is a $y$ greater than $x$” is true: after seeing $x$, choose $y = x + 1$. “There is a $y$ greater than every $x$” is false: once $y$ is fixed, $x = y$ defeats the claim. In the first statement $y$ may depend on $x$; in the second the same $y$ must work throughout. To negate the first, say that some $x$ has no greater natural $y$. This changes both quantifiers, not just the final inequality.

<Exercise question="Negate: every natural number is even.">

Some natural number is not even; 1 is a witness. Saying every natural number is odd would be a stronger and false claim.

</Exercise>
