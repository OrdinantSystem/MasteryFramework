## Negating universal statements

Red and blue cards refute both “every card is red” (blue fails) and “every card is not red” (red fails). Exact negations cannot both be false.


<span id="from-negating-universal-statements-not-every-means-some-failure"></span>

### Not every means some failure

$$
\neg(\forall x\in D\;P(x))\ \Longleftrightarrow\ \exists x\in D\;\neg P(x).
$$

Fix $D$, $P$, parameters. Classical negation reverses truth in every meaningful situation: not all pass iff some fail, possibly several, with successes remaining.

One failing member refutes universality; conversely, without failures all pass, so universality holds. This semantic proof covers infinite domains without promising a search algorithm; finite models merely illustrate.


<span id="from-negating-universal-statements-keep-the-domain-and-negate-the-whole-predicate"></span>

### Keep the domain and negate the whole predicate

$$
\neg(\forall x\in\mathbb Z\;(x>0))\ \Longleftrightarrow\ \exists x\in\mathbb Z\;(x\le0).
$$

Integer zero witnesses nonpositivity; negative one half is outside, positive integers do not fail. On positive integers, original true, negation false: correct negation need not be true.

“Red and numbered” fails by not red or not numbered (inclusive); red unnumbered suffices. Empty restricted domains, even in nonempty universes, make universals true, existential negations false, without supplying objects.

<span id="from-quantoren-und-negation-a-witness-versus-every-case"></span>

### A witness versus every case

<Math tex="\exists x\in\mathbb N:x+3=10\quad\text{(}x=7\text{)}\qquad\neg\bigl(\forall x\in\mathbb N:x+3=10\bigr)" display />

Allowed seven satisfies the equation; allowed one gives $1+3=4$, not $10$. Both displayed claims hold: success proves existence, failure refutes universality.

<span id="from-negating-existential-statements"></span>

## Negating existential statements

Drawing blue from a red–blue box leaves a red witness. Some red and some not red coexist, so are not negations.

$$
\neg(\exists x\in D\;P(x))\ \Longleftrightarrow\ \forall x\in D\;\neg P(x).
$$

Fix $D$, $P$, parameters. No witness means any candidate satisfies $\neg P$, hence all do; conversely, if all fail, none witnesses $P$. This proves both directions classically, including infinite domains.

Nonexistence needs exhaustive inspection or an arbitrary-candidate argument, not a hundred failures on an infinite domain. One failure refutes universality. Existence denies no multiplicity: even two witnesses establish it; its negation excludes all.


<span id="from-negating-existential-statements-the-restriction-stays-positive"></span>

### The restriction stays positive

$$
\neg(\exists x>0\;(x<0))\ \Longleftrightarrow\ \forall x>0\;(x\ge0).
$$

The quantifier’s $x>0$ keeps positive reals, excluding zero; only the parenthesized predicate changes. No positive real is negative: original false, denial true.

Zero witnesses an integer square zero and refutes its denial. Real squares cannot be negative by assumed arithmetic, not logic alone. Empty-domain existence is false, universal denial true, supplying no objects.

<span id="from-negating-quantified-inequalities"></span>

## Negating quantified inequalities

Exactly one unit away violates “strictly within one unit”; “more than one” misses equality. Check the boundary before quantifiers.


<span id="from-negating-quantified-inequalities-real-order-partitions-every-case"></span>

### Real order partitions every case

Trichotomy gives these complements in the usual total real order; quantifier syntax alone does not.

$$
\begin{aligned}\neg(x<c)&\Longleftrightarrow x\ge c,\\\neg(x\le c)&\Longleftrightarrow x>c,\\\neg(x>c)&\Longleftrightarrow x\le c,\\\neg(x\ge c)&\Longleftrightarrow x<c.\end{aligned}
$$

At equality, exactly one comparison holds. Negating $x=c$ gives $x\ne c$, not one direction; negating $x\ne c$ gives equality. See [order complements](../logical-negation/#from-negating-order-comparisons).


<span id="from-negating-quantified-inequalities-a-quantified-boundary-example"></span>

### A quantified boundary example

$$
\neg(\forall x>0\;(x<1))\ \Longleftrightarrow\ \exists x>0\;(x\ge1).
$$

Positive one is the boundary counterexample; zero is outside. Switch every to some, keep domain, complement comparison. Two works too; one witness suffices.


<span id="from-quantoren-und-negation-the-boundary-case-matters"></span>

### The boundary case matters

The claim $n^2 > n$ for every natural number fails at $n = 1$. With $\mathbb{N}$ starting at 1, the correct universal statement is $n^2 \ge  n$; strict inequality holds for $n > 1$.


<span id="from-negating-quantified-inequalities-when-the-predicate-is-conditional"></span>

### When the predicate is conditional

$$
\neg(A\Rightarrow B)\ \Longleftrightarrow\ A\land\neg B.
$$

| $A$ | $B$ | $A\Rightarrow B$ | $\neg(A\Rightarrow B)$ | $A\land\neg B$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| true | true | true | false | false |
| true | false | false | true | true |
| false | true | true | false | false |
| false | false | true | false | false |

Matching last columns prove equivalence over all four cases; false hypotheses cannot refute implication. Sequence failure requires the **same index** inside the tail and outside the distance bound.

<span id="from-quantoren-und-negation"></span>

## Quantifiers and negation

<span id="from-quantoren-und-negation-push-the-negation-inward"></span>

### Push the negation inward

<Math tex="\neg(\forall x\in D:P(x))\iff\exists x\in D:\neg P(x)" display />

<Math tex="\neg(\exists x\in D:P(x))\iff\forall x\in D:\neg P(x)" display />

Apply these proved laws inward in existing order, retaining domains and scope:

<Math tex="\neg(\forall x\,\exists y:P(x,y))\iff\exists x\,\forall y:\neg P(x,y)" display />


<span id="from-quantoren-und-negation-order-of-quantifiers"></span>

### Order of quantifiers

Over $\mathbb N$, $y=x+1$ supplies a larger natural for each $x$; no fixed $y$ exceeds all $x$, since $x=y$ defeats it. Negating the first says some $x$ has no larger natural: switch both quantifiers, not merely inequality.

<Exercise question="Negate: every natural number is even.">

Some natural is not even, witnessed by 1. “All odd” is stronger and false.

</Exercise>
