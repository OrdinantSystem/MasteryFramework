## Statements and predicates

<span id="from-aussagen-und-aussageformen-a-statement-can-be-false"></span>

### A statement can be false

A statement has one definite truth value, true (1) or false (0), even if unknown. Both $2+3=5$ and $2+3=6$ are statements; only the first is true.


<span id="from-aussagen-und-aussageformen-an-open-variable-leaves-a-question"></span>

### An open variable leaves a question

$P(x):x+3=10$ is a predicate depending on $x$. The domain $x\in\mathbb N$ restricts without binding. Substitution of $7$ makes it true; $2$ makes it false. Alternatively, quantify to bind $x$.

<Math tex="P(7):7+3=10\qquad P(2):2+3=10" display />

$P(2)$ is the false equality $2+3=10$, not a negated redefinition. $x=4$ also makes it false. Inputs change; the rule does not.


<span id="from-aussagen-und-aussageformen-check-your-classification"></span>

### Check your classification

Questions and commands are not statements. “This sentence is false” is a paradox without a consistent classical truth value, not an ordinary false statement. Classify before proving.


<span id="from-aussagen-und-aussageformen-substitution-and-truth"></span>

### Substitution and truth

“There exists a natural $x$ with $x+3=10$” is complete without preselecting an input; $7$ witnesses truth. A free input differs from unknown truth.

<Exercise question="Is $x^2 = 4$ a statement? What about $\exists x \in  \mathbb{N} : x^2 = 4$?">

The first is a predicate with free $x$. The second is a true statement, witnessed by $x = 2$.

</Exercise>


<span id="from-aussagen-und-aussageformen-claims-expressions-and-requests"></span>

### Claims, expressions, and requests

“The sum of two numbers,” $x+1$, and $7$ name objects, not claims. $x+1=3$ needs an input; “Seven equals seven” and “Seven is greater than zero” make claims. Symbols are neither necessary nor sufficient for propositions.

Classify the **whole utterance**: “Prove zero equals one” requests action despite its false embedded claim. “Find all reals less than one” instructs; “Every real is less than one” asserts falsely, refuted by two. Mentioning a claim does not turn a question into one.

“This number is small” needs both an object, say seven, and a threshold, say less than ten. A sealed card with a fixed integer differs from a future choice lacking value and rule: hidden is not unfixed.

In real arithmetic $1/0=2$ is undefined, not ordinarily false: no real multiplied by zero gives one, since every such product is zero. This failure of meaning is not a third truth value.


<span id="from-aussagen-und-aussageformen-truth-knowledge-and-reasons"></span>

### Truth, knowledge, and reasons

Classical **bivalence** is accepted here, not proved: every meaningful proposition has exactly one of two truth values. $T/F$ and $1$ and $0$ label truth, not arithmetic quantities.

A fixed card is zero or nonzero before opening; finding five changes knowledge, not truth. Three morning chairs versus four afternoon chairs yield different **time-indexed claims**, not two truth values for one claim. Different predicate inputs likewise complete different claims.

One witness proves existence; one counterexample refutes universality. Universal proof may require arbitrary inputs, or exhaustive checks in finite domains; selected infinite-domain successes do not suffice. For arbitrary real $x<1$, transitivity with $1<2$ yields $x<2$: logical structure alone supplies no order argument.


<span id="from-aussagen-und-aussageformen-independent-checks"></span>

### Independent checks

Classify “One equals zero,” “Explain why one equals one,” and $7$; turn $7$ into two claims. Complete $x=0$ by context and by quantification.

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>

Separate requested action from embedded claim; distinguish choosing an input from quantifying over all inputs.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Reference answer</summary>

“One equals zero” is false; “Explain …” requests action about a true equality. $7$ names a number; “Seven equals seven” and “Seven is greater than zero” claim something. Context fixing $x$ as real zero evaluates $x=0$ without binding it. “Every real equals zero” is closed and false, not an instruction.

</details>

<Exercise question="For real y in $\forall x\in\mathbb R\;(x<y)$, identify bound occurrences and the effect of assigning $y=0$. Compare $\exists x\in\mathbb R\;(x=0)$ and ‘Every integer is positive’.">

Only $x$ is bound; $y$ stays free even when assigned zero. That assignment makes the formula false, refuted by $x=1$. The existential is closed and true, witnessed by zero. The verbal universal is closed without letters and false, refuted by zero.

</Exercise>

<Exercise question="Translate ‘There is a real at least zero and less than one’; give a witness. Change ‘there is’ to ‘every’, justify the verdict, and identify what stays unchanged.">

$\exists x\in\mathbb R\;((0\le x)\land(x<1))$ is true, witnessed by zero. $\forall x\in\mathbb R\;((0\le x)\land(x<1))$ is false: one fails the strict upper bound. Both are closed with the same domain and comparisons; only the one-versus-every requirement changes.

</Exercise>

Distinguish unknown (sealed fixed card), undefined (real division by zero), and unsupported (possibly correct but inadequately justified). Reconstruct why; see [Reading mathematical structure](../quantifier-order/#from-reading-mathematical-structure).

<span id="from-definitions-assumptions-results"></span>

## Definitions, assumptions, and results

A library defines **short** loans as fewer than seven days. Three days qualifies; ten does not. These applications assert claims; a false application does not refute the chosen meaning.

Likewise, an integer is positive when $n>0$. Integers $\mathbb Z$ include negatives and zero; three qualifies, zero fails strictness. “Zero is positive” is false, not a failed definition.


<span id="from-definitions-assumptions-results-what-the-writer-is-doing"></span>

### What the writer is doing

A **definition** fixes meaning; a **hypothesis/assumption** is local to an argument; an **axiom** starts a theory. **Theorems**, **lemmas**, and textbook **Propositions** are proved results; lemmas usually support other proofs. All require the same rigor.

Logical *proposition* also covers false definite claims; the textbook heading has narrower meaning.

Locally suppose an integer exceeds five, not that every integer does. Since five exceeds zero, order transitivity makes our integer greater than zero; the definition names it positive. Order justifies; definition names.

Equality and “if and only if” can define or assert; context decides.

A **conjecture** awaits proof; a **corollary** follows from an established result. These describe roles, not degrees of truth.

<span id="from-definitions-assumptions-results-introducing-a-name"></span>

### Introducing a name

$$a:=0 \qquad a=0$$

$a:=0$ names zero; thereafter $a=0$ is true. “Define,” “suppose,” and “therefore” distinguish uses despite similar symbols.

<Note title="Notation readings">

$a:=0$: “a is defined to be zero”; $a=0$: “a equals zero”; $n>0$: “n is greater than zero”.

</Note>


<span id="from-definitions-assumptions-results-a-property-need-not-have-an-instance"></span>

### A property need not have an instance

“Greater than zero and less than zero” names a property no integer has, not an integer possessing it. “The unique object satisfying …” may require **existence** (at least one) and **uniqueness** (no second) proofs. Naming cannot supply them. Arithmetic and order remain accepted background.


<span id="from-definitions-assumptions-results-practice-with-roles-and-definitions"></span>

### Practice with roles and definitions

Explain the roles: “Call an integer nonnegative when at least zero”; “Zero is nonnegative”; “Suppose $x>0$”; “Define $b:=1$”; “Therefore $b=1$.”

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>

Which fixes meaning, assumes locally, or claims a consequence?

</details>

<details>
<summary>Reference answer</summary>

In order: definition; true application allowing equality; local hypothesis, not universal; naming; consequent equality. Definitions need no theorem-style proof, but object descriptions may require existence and uniqueness.

</details>

<Exercise question="Locally call a real admissible when at least zero and at most one. State the criterion, test zero, and explain what follows for an unspecified admissible real.">

For $x\in\mathbb R$, the criterion is $(0\le x)\land(x\le1)$. Zero passes both. Admissibility recovers both bounds, not one value: $0$, $1/2$, $1$ qualify. This local definition includes equality; strict comparisons would change it.

</Exercise>

Invent another property; separate its definition from one true and one false application. See [Several simultaneous conditions](../simultaneous-conditions/#from-simultaneous-conditions) for a longer criterion.
