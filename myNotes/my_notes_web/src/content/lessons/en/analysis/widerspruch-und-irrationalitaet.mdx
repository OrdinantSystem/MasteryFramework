## There is no greatest natural number

Assume there is a greatest natural number m. Then m + 1 is also natural and greater than m, contradicting its defining property. The contradiction targets the existence of m, not the definition of natural numbers.

## Assume √2 is rational—in lowest terms

<Math tex="\sqrt2=\frac pq,\qquad p,q\in\mathbb Z,\quad q>0,\quad\gcd(|p|,q)=1" display />

Every rational number admits a reduced fraction. Choosing that representation is essential: merely discovering that some fraction can be reduced would not refute rationality.

## Both integers must be even

<Math tex="p^2=2q^2\Longrightarrow p=2k\Longrightarrow4k^2=2q^2\Longrightarrow q^2=2k^2\Longrightarrow q=2\ell" display />

The parity lemma proves each implication from an even square to an even integer. Thus 2 divides both p and q, contradicting their coprimality. Therefore √2 is irrational. An alternative proof uses infinite descent: repeated halving would produce smaller and smaller positive integer denominators, which cannot continue indefinitely.

## The two uses of parity

First p² = 2q² makes p² even. The parity result then makes p even, so we may introduce an integer k with p = 2k. Substitution gives 4k² = 2q²; dividing by 2 makes q² even, and the same result makes q even. We have not contradicted the equation alone: fractions such as 2/4 can have two even entries. We contradict the initial choice of a fraction with no common factor. That is why the reduced-fraction assumption must be stated before the calculation.

<Exercise question="Why is “p² even, therefore p even” not an unexplained algebraic step?">

It invokes the previously proved parity equivalence. Without that result, the proof would need to establish that an odd integer has an odd square.

</Exercise>
