## What a proof establishes

Justify proof steps from definitions, assumptions or established results; examples alone do not prove universal claims.

$\sqrt2$ is the positive real with square $2$. We prove it **irrational**: not a ratio of integers. A decimal approximation such as $1.4142$ cannot exclude every fraction.

For **contradiction**, assume a fraction exists and derive incompatible properties of that same fraction.


<span id="from-widerspruch-und-irrationalitaet-a-first-proof-by-contradiction"></span>

## A first proof by contradiction

Consider the claim: **there is no greatest natural number**.

1. Suppose a greatest natural $m$ exists, so every natural is at most $m$.
2. Its successor $m+1$ is natural.
3. But $m+1>m$, contradicting maximality.
4. Therefore no greatest natural exists, whether naturals start at $0$ or $1$.

The same $m+1$ would be both at most and greater than $m$. A contradiction needs incompatibility, not merely surprise.


<span id="from-widerspruch-und-irrationalitaet-the-parity-fact-we-will-need"></span>

## The parity fact we will need

[The parity lesson](../beweissprache-und-methoden/#from-beweissprache-und-methoden) proves that an integer is even exactly when its square is even, using the odd form for the reverse contrapositive. We apply that reverse implication twice, not an assumed divisibility rule for square roots.

## Why $\sqrt{2}$ cannot be a fraction

<span id="from-widerspruch-und-irrationalitaet-1-assume-a-fraction-exists-and-reduce-it"></span>

### 1. Assume a fraction exists and reduce it

Assume $\sqrt2$ rational. Its integer fraction has nonzero denominator; choose the denominator positive and cancel all common factors:

<Math tex="\sqrt2=\frac pq,\qquad p,q\in\mathbb Z,\quad q>0,\quad\gcd(|p|,q)=1" display />

$p$ is the numerator, $q$ the denominator. **Lowest terms** means $\gcd(|p|,q)=1$: no integer greater than $1$ divides both. Cancellation preserves value, so every rational permits this choice. Positivity gives $p>0$.

<span id="from-widerspruch-und-irrationalitaet-2-turn-the-assumed-equality-into-an-integer-equation"></span>

### 2. Turn the assumed equality into an integer equation

Square, then multiply by nonzero $q^2$:

<Math tex="2=\frac{p^2}{q^2},\qquad 2q^2=p^2" display />

This integer equation lets us use divisibility.

<span id="from-widerspruch-und-irrationalitaet-3-show-that-the-numerator-is-even"></span>

### 3. Show that the numerator is even

$p^2=2q^2$ makes $p^2$ even because $q^2$ is an integer. The parity lemma makes $p$ even.

Thus $p=2k$ for an integer $k$, by definition, not an extra assumption.

<span id="from-widerspruch-und-irrationalitaet-4-substitute-to-learn-about-the-denominator"></span>

### 4. Substitute to learn about the denominator

Substitute $p=2k$ into $p^2=2q^2$:

<Math tex="(2k)^2=2q^2,\qquad 4k^2=2q^2,\qquad q^2=2k^2" display />

Dividing by $2$ gives $q^2=2k^2$, so $q^2$ is even. The parity lemma now makes $q$ even.

<span id="from-widerspruch-und-irrationalitaet-5-identify-the-contradiction"></span>

### 5. Identify the contradiction

Thus $2$ divides both $p$ and $q$: their greatest common divisor is at least $2$, contradicting its chosen value $1$.

**The contradiction requires lowest terms.** Two even entries alone are possible: $2/4$ is valid but reducible.

<span id="from-widerspruch-und-irrationalitaet-6-state-what-follows"></span>

### 6. State what follows

Every rational has a lowest-terms representation, so no alternative representation escapes the contradiction. Hence $\sqrt2$ is irrational.


<span id="from-widerspruch-und-irrationalitaet-how-the-proof-methods-fit-together"></span>

## How the proof methods fit together

The overall strategy is contradiction; its supporting parity lemma uses contraposition.

| Method | What you do | Its role in this example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Direct proof | Start from the hypotheses and derive the conclusion. | Substituting the odd form and expanding directly proves that an odd integer has an odd square. |
| Contraposition | To prove $A\Rightarrow B$, prove $\neg B\Rightarrow\neg A$. | The odd-square calculation establishes that an even square must have an even integer as its root. |
| Contradiction | Assume the negation of the goal and derive incompatible statements. | The assumed reduced fraction for $\sqrt2$ must also have a common factor $2$. |
| Infinite descent | Show that any proposed positive-integer solution would yield a strictly smaller one. | The same parity argument would let us halve both entries of a proposed fraction again and again. |

For **descent**, start with any supposed fraction for $\sqrt2$, not necessarily reduced. Parity makes both entries even. Halving preserves integer entries and value but strictly decreases the positive denominator. Repeating would give endlessly decreasing positive integers, impossible because every nonempty set of positive integers has a least member. Lowest terms instead gives an immediate contradiction at the first common factor.

A **counterexample** refutes a universal claim: $\sqrt4=2$ refutes “every positive integer has irrational square root,” not the specific $\sqrt2$ claim. **Induction** uses an initial case and successor step for a natural-indexed family; it is unnecessary here.


<span id="from-widerspruch-und-irrationalitaet-check-the-reasoning"></span>

## Check the reasoning

<Exercise question="‘Both p and q are even, so $\sqrt{2}$ is irrational.’ Which earlier choice is needed, and why?">

Choose lowest terms. Even entries merely allow reduction; they contradict the chosen absence of a common divisor greater than $1$, since both have divisor $2$.

</Exercise>

<Exercise question="Try the same argument for $\sqrt{4}$. At which step does the reasoning for $\sqrt{2}$ stop working?">

From $p^2=4q^2$, parity gives $p=2k$. Substitution yields $4k^2=4q^2$, hence $k^2=q^2$, which does not force $q^2$ even. Indeed $\sqrt4=2/1$ is reduced. The original factor $2$ is needed to force both entries even.

</Exercise>
