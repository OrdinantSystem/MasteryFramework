## What a proof establishes

A mathematical proof explains why a precisely stated claim must follow from definitions, assumptions, and results already established. Each step needs a reason. Calculating many examples can suggest a pattern, but it does not establish a claim about every possible case.

Our main claim is that $\sqrt2$ is **irrational**: it is a real number that cannot be written as a ratio of two integers. Here $\sqrt2$ means the positive real number whose square is $2$. A decimal approximation such as $1.4142$ cannot settle the claim: a finite calculation cannot rule out every possible fraction.

We will use **proof by contradiction**. We temporarily assume the opposite—that such a fraction exists—and show that this assumption forces two incompatible properties of the same fraction.


<span id="from-widerspruch-und-irrationalitaet-a-first-proof-by-contradiction"></span>

## A first proof by contradiction

Consider the claim: **there is no greatest natural number**.

1. **Assume the opposite.** Suppose there is a greatest natural number, and call it $m$. This means every natural number must be at most $m$.
2. **Construct another permitted number.** The successor $m+1$ is also a natural number.
3. **Compare it with the assumed greatest number.** Since $m+1>m$, we have found a natural number greater than $m$. This contradicts the property used to choose $m$.
4. **Reject the temporary assumption.** No natural number can be greatest. The argument works whether the convention for natural numbers includes $0$ or starts at $1$.

The contradiction is specific: the same number $m+1$ would have to be both at most $m$ and greater than $m$. A proof by contradiction must identify an incompatibility of this kind; reaching an unexpected result is not enough.


<span id="from-widerspruch-und-irrationalitaet-the-parity-fact-we-will-need"></span>

## The parity fact we will need

[Proof methods and parity](../beweissprache-und-methoden/#from-beweissprache-und-methoden) proves both directions: an integer is even if and only if its square is even. Its reverse direction uses contraposition, expanding an arbitrary odd integer as twice an integer plus one. We will apply that established reverse implication twice below. We are not assuming that taking a square root preserves divisibility.

<span id="from-widerspruch-und-irrationalitaet-why-2sqrt22-cannot-be-a-fraction"></span>

## Why $\sqrt{2}$ cannot be a fraction

<span id="from-widerspruch-und-irrationalitaet-1-assume-a-fraction-exists-and-reduce-it"></span>

### 1. Assume a fraction exists and reduce it

Suppose, for contradiction, that $\sqrt2$ is rational. By the definition of rationality, it can be written as a fraction of integers with nonzero denominator. We can choose a positive denominator and cancel every common factor, giving

<Math tex="\sqrt2=\frac pq,\qquad p,q\in\mathbb Z,\quad q>0,\quad\gcd(|p|,q)=1" display />

Here $p$ is the numerator and $q$ is the denominator. The notation $\gcd(|p|,q)=1$ says their greatest common divisor is $1$: no integer greater than $1$ divides both. This is what **in lowest terms** means. Cancelling common factors changes the representation, not the number, so this choice is allowed for every rational number. Since $\sqrt2>0$ and $q>0$, we also have $p>0$.

<span id="from-widerspruch-und-irrationalitaet-2-turn-the-assumed-equality-into-an-integer-equation"></span>

### 2. Turn the assumed equality into an integer equation

Square both sides. The square of $\sqrt2$ is $2$, and the square of $p/q$ is $p^2/q^2$. Then multiply both sides by $q^2$, which is nonzero:

<Math tex="2=\frac{p^2}{q^2},\qquad 2q^2=p^2" display />

We now have an equation between integers. This makes divisibility by $2$ available as a tool.

<span id="from-widerspruch-und-irrationalitaet-3-show-that-the-numerator-is-even"></span>

### 3. Show that the numerator is even

The equation $p^2=2q^2$ writes $p^2$ as twice the integer $q^2$. By definition, $p^2$ is even. The parity result from the preceding proof lesson then tells us that $p$ itself is even.

Therefore there is an integer $k$ such that $p=2k$. Introducing $k$ simply expresses the definition of evenness; it adds no new assumption.

<span id="from-widerspruch-und-irrationalitaet-4-substitute-to-learn-about-the-denominator"></span>

### 4. Substitute to learn about the denominator

Replace $p$ by $2k$ in $p^2=2q^2$. Squaring $2k$ gives $4k^2$, so

<Math tex="(2k)^2=2q^2,\qquad 4k^2=2q^2,\qquad q^2=2k^2" display />

The last equality follows by dividing both sides by $2$ and reversing the sides. It writes $q^2$ as twice the integer $k^2$. Hence $q^2$ is even. Applying the same parity fact again, $q$ must be even too.

<span id="from-widerspruch-und-irrationalitaet-5-identify-the-contradiction"></span>

### 5. Identify the contradiction

We have proved that $p$ and $q$ are both even. Thus $2$ divides both, and their greatest common divisor is at least $2$. But we chose the fraction so that this divisor was $1$.

These properties cannot both hold. **The contradiction is between the reduced-fraction condition and the common factor $2$ forced by the equation.** Two even entries alone are not impossible: $2/4$ is a valid fraction, but it is not in lowest terms.

<span id="from-widerspruch-und-irrationalitaet-6-state-what-follows"></span>

### 6. State what follows

The assumption that $\sqrt2$ has a rational representation led to a contradiction. Every rational number has a representation in lowest terms, so there is no other rational representation left to try. Therefore $\sqrt2$ is irrational.


<span id="from-widerspruch-und-irrationalitaet-how-the-proof-methods-fit-together"></span>

## How the proof methods fit together

A proof can use one overall strategy and another method for a supporting result. Here the overall argument is by contradiction, while its parity lemma is proved by contraposition.

| Method | What you do | Its role in this example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Direct proof | Start from the hypotheses and derive the conclusion. | Substituting the odd form and expanding directly proves that an odd integer has an odd square. |
| Contraposition | To prove $A\Rightarrow B$, prove $\neg B\Rightarrow\neg A$. | The odd-square calculation establishes that an even square must have an even integer as its root. |
| Contradiction | Assume the negation of the goal and derive incompatible statements. | The assumed reduced fraction for $\sqrt2$ must also have a common factor $2$. |
| Infinite descent | Show that any proposed positive-integer solution would yield a strictly smaller one. | The same parity argument would let us halve both entries of a proposed fraction again and again. |

For the descent version, start with any supposed fraction for $\sqrt2$, without requiring lowest terms. The argument above makes both entries even, so halving them gives integers representing the same number, with a smaller positive denominator. The argument applies again to this new fraction. That would produce an endless strictly decreasing sequence of positive integer denominators, which is impossible: every nonempty set of positive integers has a least element. The reduced-fraction proof reaches the contradiction as soon as the first common factor is found.

A **counterexample** serves a different task: it refutes a universal claim. For example, $\sqrt4=2$ refutes “the square root of every positive integer is irrational”. It does not refute the specific claim about $\sqrt2$. **Induction** proves a family of statements indexed by natural numbers using a starting case and a successor step; it is not needed for this single claim.


<span id="from-widerspruch-und-irrationalitaet-check-the-reasoning"></span>

## Check the reasoning

<Exercise question="A proposed proof ends with: ‘p and q are both even, so $\sqrt{2}$ is irrational.’ What earlier choice must it state, and why?">

It must choose the fraction in lowest terms. Both entries being even means that the fraction can be reduced; that alone is not impossible. The contradiction appears because a fraction chosen with no common divisor greater than $1$ has been shown to have the common divisor $2$.

</Exercise>

<Exercise question="Try the same argument for $\sqrt{4}$. At which step does the reasoning for $\sqrt{2}$ stop working?">

Squaring a supposed fraction gives $p^2=4q^2$. This makes $p$ even, so write $p=2k$. Substitution gives $4k^2=4q^2$, hence $k^2=q^2$. This does not force $q^2$ to be even. There is no contradiction: $\sqrt4=2/1$ is already a reduced fraction. The factor $2$ in the original equation is essential to forcing both entries to be even.

</Exercise>
