“At least three” includes three. Its negation must exclude three, so “at most three” cannot be right: both would hold at the boundary. The correct denial is “strictly less than three.”

Let $x$ and $c$ be real numbers in their usual order. Here $c$ is the boundary. The following are derived rules, not four unrelated replacements to memorize:

$$\neg(x>c)\Leftrightarrow x\le c$$
$$\neg(x\ge c)\Leftrightarrow x<c$$
$$\neg(x<c)\Leftrightarrow x\ge c$$
$$\neg(x\le c)\Leftrightarrow x>c$$

In each line $\Leftrightarrow$ says that both conditions agree for every allowed assignment. Direction changes, and the inclusion of equality changes too.

## Proof from trichotomy

Fix any real input $x$ and boundary $c$. The usual real order places $x$ in exactly one of three positions: below $c$, equal to $c$, or above $c$. This property, **trichotomy**, is accepted as foundational order background.

- “Above” is true only in the above position. Its negation selects below and equal: $x\le c$.
- “Above or equal” selects two positions. Its negation selects only below: $x<c$.
- “Below” selects one position. Its negation selects equal and above: $x\ge c$.
- “Below or equal” selects two positions. Its negation selects only above: $x>c$.

Every real input has exactly one of these positions, and nothing depended on a special numerical choice. This proves all four laws. Checking $2,3,4$ around the boundary three illustrates them and can expose errors, but three samples alone do not prove a law for infinitely many real inputs. These rules need not hold unchanged for arbitrary partial orders, where incomparable objects may occur.

## Read the full comparison and keep the domain

The negation of $0<x$ is $0\ge x$, equivalently $x\le0$. Read the input on the right as carefully as one on the left. Equality belongs in the answer because zero fails the original strict comparison.

If $x$ is restricted to **nonnegative reals**, denying $x>3$ gives $x\le3$ in that same domain: exactly the inputs in $[0,3]$. Negative one is not allowed, even though it satisfies the comparison on the larger real line. No quantifier is added, and a free $x$ stays free. Negating is not an inequality-solving operation or permission to change the universe of inputs.

## Boundary practice

Negate $x>3$, $x\ge3$, $x<3$, and $x\le3$. Give the truth values of both originals and denials at $x=3$.

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>

At equality, the strict originals are false and the non-strict originals are true. Reverse those verdicts.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Reference answer</summary>

In order: $x\le3$, $x<3$, $x\ge3$, $x>3$. At three the originals have values $0,1,0,1$ and the denials $1,0,1,0$. Changing direction without changing equality inclusion fails this check.

</details>

The first answer is $x>5$. Five satisfies the original, so it must fail the denial. The second is $x\le0$ in the nonnegative domain; only $x=0$ satisfies it. Negative inputs have not become permitted. These conclusions concern the same inputs and boundaries as their originals.
