## Real sequences

The device reports nine twice, then three forever. In “third report equals three,” position and value coincide numerically but have different roles.


<span id="from-real-sequences-one-output-at-every-positive-index"></span>

### One output at every positive index

$$
\mathbb N=\{1,2,3,\ldots\},\qquad a:\mathbb N\to\mathbb R,\qquad a_n=a(n).
$$

A real sequence assigns one real term to every positive natural index. Zero is excluded here; specify other conventions. Here $n$ is the index, $a_n$ is the term at that index, and $a$ is the whole function. Subscripts do not multiply; the arrow specifies domains, not convergence.

$$
(a_n)_{n=1}^{\infty}.
$$

There is no last term or index infinity. Positive natural indices can output negative, zero or positive values.


<span id="from-real-sequences-a-rule-must-specify-every-position"></span>

### A rule must specify every position

$$
a_n=2n\quad(n\in\mathbb N).
$$

Thus $a_1=2$, $a_2=4$, $a_3=6$. Constant $a_n=c$ fixes one real output, for example $-3$. The device rule specifies $a_1=a_2=9$ and $a_n=3$ for all $n\ge3$; its three-term prefix alone permits infinitely many continuations.

Unlike sets, sequences retain positions and repetitions. Alternating zero–one and one–zero share values but differ at index one. Equality requires agreement at every index, not a finite prefix.

A tail includes $N$ and all later indices: from $N=3$ the device gives only threes. Both $N$ and $n$ are natural numbers: $N$ starts the tail, and $n$ is the index being tested. Zero then always one agrees with constant one from two onward, but is unequal to it.

## Absolute value and distance

Minus three and plus three both have distance three from zero. Absolute value measures nonnegative distance, not direction.

$$
|x|=\begin{cases}x,&x\ge0,\\-x,&x<0.\end{cases}
$$

Each real belongs to one branch. For $x<0$, $-x$ is positive: $|-3|=|3|=3$, $|0|=0$. Nonnegative includes zero. Familiar real arithmetic and order are assumed.


<span id="from-absolute-value-and-distance-signed-displacement-and-distance"></span>

### Signed displacement and distance

$$
d(x,y)=|x-y|,\qquad |2-5|=|-3|=3,\qquad |5-2|=3.
$$

$x-y$ is displacement from $y$ to $x$; absolute value gives length. Reversing subtraction negates displacement. A number and its opposite have equal absolute values. For a positive input, both absolute values equal that input; for a negative input, both equal its positive opposite; at zero, both equal zero. Hence $d(x,y)=d(y,x)$ universally.

Zero distance is equivalent to $x=y$: a nonnegative difference equals its absolute value; a negative difference has positive absolute value. Thus zero distance forces zero difference, and conversely. No triangle inequality is needed.


<span id="from-absolute-value-and-distance-a-strict-tolerance-is-an-open-interval"></span>

### A strict tolerance is an open interval

$$
\varepsilon>0:\qquad |t-L|<\varepsilon\ \Longleftrightarrow\ L-\varepsilon<t<L+\varepsilon.
$$

For fixed $L$, $\varepsilon>0$, put $u=t-L$. If $u\ge0$, distance below tolerance means $u<\varepsilon$; $-\varepsilon<u$ is automatic. If $u<0$, it means $-u<\varepsilon$, equivalently $-\varepsilon<u$; the upper bound is automatic. Thus $-\varepsilon<u<\varepsilon$; add $L$. Reversibility proves both directions.

Both bounds are strict: at target three, tolerance $1/2$, endpoints $2.5,3.5$ fail by equality. Epsilon varies, rather than being a fixed tiny constant.
