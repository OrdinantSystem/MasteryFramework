## What a mind map represents

When several ideas seem connected but you cannot explain how, putting them on paper can help you inspect the confusion. You have to decide what belongs together, which ideas depend on others, and where a connection is still uncertain. The transcript emphasizes this work of organizing and comparing. It gives no complete drawing algorithm; the procedure here is our practical interpretation.

Start with a question small enough to answer. “How can I decide whether a map is linear?” calls for definitions, tests, and counterexamples. A map about calculating outputs would organize the same subject differently. Choose the relationships that serve your question.

## From a list of words to relationships

Read the arrows in the derivative example as sentences. A derivative is the limit of difference quotients, when that limit exists. Tangent slope and instantaneous rate explain what the result means in suitable settings. The limit is the operation connecting the ideas. Simply writing “limit” beside “derivative” would leave that connection for you to guess.

## Worked example: linear maps

Suppose your first list contains addition, scaling, zero, matrices, and straight lines. Put addition and scaling beside the definition of a linear map. Before drawing an arrow to zero, work out what it claims.

<RelationMap />

Additivity gives f(0) = f(0) + f(0). Subtract f(0) to obtain f(0) = 0. You can therefore label the arrow from linearity to the zero condition “implies”. The reverse arrow fails: x² sends zero to zero but does not preserve addition. Keeping this counterexample beside the failed arrow prevents the drawing from suggesting an equivalence.

“Matrix” also needs a precise link: after bases are chosen, a linear map between finite-dimensional spaces has a matrix representation. “Straight line” needs more care. A translated straight-line graph can represent an affine function that is not linear. If you cannot yet explain a proposed connection, mark it as a question to investigate.

## Constructing and checking a map

1. Write the question the map should help answer, and leave unrelated material aside.
2. Recall a few central ideas before consulting the source. Mark what you are unsure about.
3. Group definitions, consequences, examples, and methods. Move them when a different arrangement makes a dependency clearer.
4. Give each important arrow a direction and a phrase such as “implies”, “requires”, or “is an example of”. Read the whole connection aloud as a sentence.
5. Supply a reason for that sentence. Add a condition or counterexample if it changes the claim.
6. Check against the course source and correct the relationships as well as the labels.
7. Put the map away. Rebuild its core from memory, then use one relationship in a problem.

The resulting map may have crossed-out arrows. Those corrections record decisions you can explain. Stop adding detail when it no longer helps answer the original question; a few well-justified relationships can be enough for the session.

<Exercise question="A map contains the arrow ‘f(0) = 0 → f is linear’. How would you repair it and explain the change?">

Reverse the arrow: linearity implies f(0) = 0. Label the zero condition as necessary, and keep x² as a counterexample to the original implication. To establish linearity, you must check addition and scaling throughout the domain. The zero test can rule a map out, but cannot certify one by itself.

</Exercise>

Mapping can help you organize a subject, while rebuilding the map tests what you can retrieve. In [Karpicke and Blunt (2011)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21252317/), retrieval outperformed the particular concept-mapping activity studied. That finding is a reason to retain retrieval in your practice, rather than assume the drawing itself establishes learning.
