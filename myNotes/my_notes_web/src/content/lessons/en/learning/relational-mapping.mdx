## Give each important link a meaning

Imagine looking at a map labeled “derivative, slope, limit, rules”. You recognize the words, but the lines do not yet tell you how they fit. Replace a vague line with a short proposition: a derivative is the limit of a difference quotient, when that limit exists. Now there is something to explain, verify, and challenge.

## Build a small map yourself

1. Choose one central question and its boundaries.
2. List the main ideas from memory, then check the source.
3. Label links: requires, implies, is an example of, or fails without.
4. Explain the most important links and look for missing assumptions.
5. Hide the map and rebuild its core later.

A few justified relationships are more useful here than a large, polished drawing you cannot reconstruct.

Mapping does not replace retrieval. In [Karpicke and Blunt’s study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21252317/), retrieval practice outperformed the tested concept-mapping activity, including on inference questions. Use maps to organize and test relationships, and still practise recalling and applying them.
