## Learning objectives

You can spend an evening reading about linear maps and still hesitate when someone asks whether a particular map is linear. Before you start, put the task into words: “Given a map I have not seen before, I want to decide whether it is linear and justify my answer.” That gives you something to practise and a way to notice progress. Your course notes supply the definitions and conventions you will use.

## The four methods

<LearningFigure kind="methods" />

Each method helps with a different part of the work. A relationship map makes you decide how ideas fit together. Explaining from memory exposes places where you cannot yet supply the reasoning. Mixed problems ask you to choose a method for yourself. Spaced retrieval brings important knowledge back into use after a delay.

You can combine them without including a map, an explanation, a problem set, and a stack of cards in every session. If you know the relationships but keep forgetting a theorem’s conditions, practise recalling those conditions. If you remember the theorem yet cannot recognize when to use it, spend the time on problems that require that choice.

## The learning cycle

<ProcessDiagram preset="cycle" id="learning-cycle-diagram" />

Read the return path as permission to work on the piece that failed. A missing prerequisite may need a short explanation and a worked example before you can make a useful attempt. A mistaken inference needs closer attention to that inference. You need not restart the whole topic.

This cycle is our synthesis of the supplied sources, offered as a workflow to test and adjust. Research on individual practices does not establish this particular sequence as optimal. After an immediate success, leave a different task for a later session so you can check what remains available.

## Example: linearity

Suppose you can recite the definition but call g(x) = 2x + 3 linear because its graph is straight. Start by testing zero: g(0) = 3, whereas a linear map must send zero to zero. Then explain how that condition follows from preserving addition. Your map can now connect a definition to a necessary consequence, with a reason for the arrow.

For the next attempt, use a different map and check addition and scaling. Passing the zero test alone does not establish linearity. Keep a note of whether you reached that conclusion independently or needed the reminder, and return to the distinction after a delay.
