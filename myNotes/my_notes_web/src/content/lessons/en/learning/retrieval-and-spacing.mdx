## Retrieval before checking

You may recognize every line of a proof while reading and still be unable to write its first step with the page closed. To find out what you can retrieve, hide the notes and attempt a definition, explanation, or problem before checking the answer.

If the attempt fails, open the source and study the missing part. Reading has a useful job here: it supplies or corrects knowledge you could not produce. Then close it and try again. Keep track of whether an answer came from memory or from the page in front of you.

## Review intervals

<LearningFigure kind="spacing" />

The source protocol suggests brief reviews on the same day, the next day, after 3–4 days, around a week, and after 21–30 days. Use these as starting examples, not a timetable you must obey. Shorten a gap after difficulty and lengthen it after reliable independent success. Deadlines and a manageable workload matter too.

Change the task as well as the date. Recall a theorem’s assumptions in one review; later solve a problem without its name in the heading. That helps distinguish remembering a familiar answer from having knowledge available for use.

## Flashcard design

A card works best when it asks one clear question and gives you an answer you can check. Exact definitions, theorem conditions, brief relationships, small counterexamples, and short applications can fit. Make cards after you have begun to understand the material, and rewrite cards that have become vague or overloaded. Keep full proofs and longer problems in your problem-solving practice.

Practice testing and distributed practice received high utility ratings in [Dunlosky et al.’s 2013 review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26173288/). That supports the component practices, without validating our exact review intervals or complete workflow.

## Example: a card and a problem

Replace “Do I understand contraposition?” with “State the contrapositive of $A \Rightarrow  B$.” You can check the answer $\neg B \Rightarrow  \neg A$. A different card can ask for a counterexample to “every even integer is divisible by 4”; 6 works because it is even but not divisible by 4.

Neither card asks you to decide when contraposition would be useful. Leave a separate, unlabeled proof task for that decision. If you recall the card easily but cannot choose the method, add selection practice rather than simply increasing repetitions of the same card.
