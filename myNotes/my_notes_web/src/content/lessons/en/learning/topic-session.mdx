## Preparation

Choose a task you want to complete independently by the end. For example: “Prove a short claim about even and odd integers, using the definitions.” Set aside the time you have and identify the course source you will consult. Two or three short questions, or one small problem, can show which prerequisites need attention before you continue.

## Session structure

1. Attempt a small task without notes or hints. Keep the answer so you can distinguish what you knew at the start from what you learned during the session.
2. Sketch the few definitions and relationships the task needs. Leave uncertain links open for checking.
3. Work from an example to the precise statement. If you are new to the material, study a worked solution and explain the reasons behind its steps.
4. Attempt problems with gradually less support. Give independent work enough time; following an explanation should not occupy the whole session.
5. Locate a specific mistake, repair it, and try a different case without the earlier hint.
6. Finish with closed-notes recall and a new application. Record what you could do, where help was needed, and what remains untested.
7. Leave a concrete task for a later review.

The sequence is adjustable. You can spend most of a session repairing a prerequisite if it blocks the rest. A diagram or extra exercise is useful only when it helps with the work in front of you.

## Time and difficulty

The sources include 75-, 90-, and 120-minute examples. None is a required duration or an established optimum. For a proof-heavy topic, allow more time for your own arguments. Shorten mapping when the relationships are already clear. If fatigue makes further attempts unproductive, stop with a specific next task rather than prolonging the session to finish a checklist.

The general steps above can be used with a familiar question from your own subject. The proof session below is a mathematical illustration. Bloom's familiar task comparison can help name what you are practising; you need not complete its convergence example first.

## Example session

{/* Complete reference reasoning added for the existing parity tasks. */}

An integer is **even** if it is $2k$ for some integer $k$, and **odd** if it is $2k+1$. Every integer is exactly one of even or odd. Start by writing those definitions; if they are unavailable, study them before attempting a proof.

Here is the worked argument for an odd square. Take any odd integer $n=2k+1$. Then $n^2=4k^2+4k+1=2(2k^2+2k)+1$. Since $2k^2+2k$ is an integer, the square has the form twice an integer plus one, so it is odd. Hide the argument and reconstruct it. Record this as reconstruction after study.

You have now seen a closely related proof. Keep that exposure separate from hints used during the following changed immediate application; it does not establish unfamiliar strategy selection. Do not ask for a method name at the outset.

<Exercise question="If an integer’s square is even, the integer is even. Prove this claim.">

Suppose the integer were odd. The worked argument shows that its square would be odd, contradicting the assumption that the square is even. Since every integer is exactly one of odd or even, the integer must be even. This is contraposition: we used “odd integer gives odd square” to establish “even square gives even integer”.

</Exercise>

<Exercise question="Why does 6 refute ‘every even integer is divisible by 4’ ?">

$6=2\cdot3$ is even, but $6/4=3/2$ is not an integer, so 6 is not divisible by 4. One allowed input satisfies the hypothesis and fails the conclusion; that refutes the universal claim.

</Exercise>

For a later session, leave this changed parity task. Record when you attempt it and any help. Opening the answer beforehand makes a later repetition reconstruction; choose another unseen task if you want to check independent work.

<Exercise question="Can the square of an even integer be odd? Justify your answer for every even integer, not just one example.">

No. Write an arbitrary even integer as $n=2k$, with integer $k$. Then $n^2=4k^2=2(2k^2)$. Since $2k^2$ is integer, the square is even and therefore cannot be odd.

</Exercise>
