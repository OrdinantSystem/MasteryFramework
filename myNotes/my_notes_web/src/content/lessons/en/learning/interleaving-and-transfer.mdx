## Blocked and mixed practice

A page titled “Use contraposition” makes one decision before you even read the problem. That can help when you are learning the technique. Later, however, you need practice deciding whether contraposition fits at all.

<LearningFigure kind="interleaving" />

Interleaving mixes related problem types so you must recognize the relevant conditions and choose an approach. Begin with basic examples of each type if they are new. Once you can attempt them, remove the method labels and mix the order. Switching at random between unrelated subjects may add interruptions without giving you this particular practice in choosing.

## Comparison tasks

Compare the real sequences $1 - 1/n$, $n$, and $(-1)^n$. The first is bounded, increasing, and convergent. The second increases without bound. The third is bounded but alternates. Explaining those differences helps you see why “bounded” and “monotone” cannot be substituted for each other in a theorem.

You can vary representations, ask for a counterexample, or change a hypothesis as well. These activities complement mixed practice. The source’s broad use of “interleaving” includes such variations, but comparison, varied retrieval, and transfer are not all identical to interleaving as studied in research.

## One claim from two directions

Consider the claim “if $n$ is odd, then $n + 1$ is even.” A direct proof writes $n=2k+1$, so $n+1=2(k+1)$. A proof by contraposition starts by assuming $n+1$ is odd: $n+1=2k+1$ implies $n=2k$, so $n$ is even. Both routes establish the same claim, but start from different information. Choosing a method means asking which starting point makes the argument simpler.

Interleaving gives you repeated opportunities to make such choices across related problems. Explaining one claim algebraically, drawing it, and teaching it to someone can deepen understanding too; those changes of perspective are complementary activities rather than automatically a blocked-versus-interleaved comparison.

## What the studies show

[Rohrer, Dedrick & Burgess (2014)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24578089/) studied 140 seventh-grade students over nine weeks. On an unannounced test two weeks later, mean scores were 72% for interleaved material and 38% for blocked material. This is a result for those mathematics tasks, not a predicted gain for the proof example above.

[Brunmair & Richter (2019)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31556629/) synthesized 59 studies. The overall benefit was moderate, the mathematics benefit smaller, and results varied by material; word-learning tasks favored blocking. Mixing everything is therefore not a universal recipe.

For your own practice, learn an initial example of each method, then mix related tasks without strategy labels. Explain your choice and check it against feedback. Revisit fresh problems later: successful repetition immediately after an example does not yet show that you can choose independently.

Whether you can select the strategy. Attempt a different problem of suitable difficulty with no theorem named. State which hypotheses make your chosen result applicable. If you need another hint, record it; being able to carry out a method and being able to choose it are both worth practising.
