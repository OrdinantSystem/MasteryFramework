## The six categories

<QuestionLens />

Remember, understand, apply, analyze, evaluate, and create describe cognitive processes. They are not fixed stages to complete in order, nor a score for how valuable your thinking is. A proof can require several at once. Creating can mean constructing your own valid example; it need not mean original scientific research.

## Problem-oriented study

<LearningFigure kind="problem-first" />

For example: how could you prove a real sequence converges without knowing its limit? That question gives you a reason to learn boundedness, monotonicity, and the monotone convergence theorem. If you cannot find a first step, ask for a prerequisite or a worked example. The question orients study; it does not require discovering the theorem alone.

<Exercise question="An increasing sequence must converge. What is missing?">

An upper bound is missing. The real sequence aₙ = n increases but does not converge to a real number. Boundedness alone also does not suffice: (−1)ⁿ is bounded and does not converge. The theorem needs both monotonicity and boundedness.

</Exercise>

## A worked comparison

For aₙ = 1 − 1/n, applying a theorem requires two checks: the terms increase because aₙ₊₁ − aₙ = 1/[n(n + 1)] > 0, and 0 ≤ aₙ < 1 for n ≥ 1. Monotonicity and boundedness establish convergence in ℝ. Analyzing asks why n and (−1)ⁿ fail one of these conditions. Evaluating asks whether this theorem or a direct limit proof better answers the particular question. The labels describe different work on the same example; merely adding “why” to a recall question does not make it analysis.
