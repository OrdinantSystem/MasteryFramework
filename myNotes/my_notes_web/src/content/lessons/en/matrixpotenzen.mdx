## What does J do?

The matrix $J$ swaps the coordinates and changes one sign:

<Math tex="J=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad J\binom xy=\binom{-y}x." display />

Geometrically, it rotates by $90^\circ$ counterclockwise. The origin stays fixed, and lengths and angles are preserved.

<Explorer kind="rotation" />

## The first powers

$J^n$ means $n$ successive applications. $J^0=I_2$ is the identity: no rotation.

| Power | Matrix | Geometric effect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $J^0$ | $I_2$ | Starting position |
| $J^1$ | $J$ | $90^\circ$ |
| $J^2$ | $-I_2$ | $180^\circ$ |
| $J^3$ | $-J$ | $270^\circ$ |
| $J^4$ | $I_2$ | $360^\circ$, back to the start |

For example, multiplication gives:

<Math tex="J^2=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}-1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}=-I_2." display />

## Why the cycle repeats after four steps

Since $J^4=I_2$, the exponent law gives, for every integer $n\ge0$:

<Math tex="J^{n+4}=J^nJ^4=J^nI_2=J^n." display />

This proves the observed repetition. To evaluate a large power, only the remainder of the exponent after division by $4$ matters.

<Exercise question="Which matrix is J²⁰²⁶? Try reducing the exponent first.">
Since $2026=4\cdot506+2$, we have $J^{2026}=J^2=-I_2$. Every vector is mapped to its negative.
</Exercise>
