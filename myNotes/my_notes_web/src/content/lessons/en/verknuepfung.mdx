## Read the chain from the right

The second exercise combines three linear substitutions. First, $B$ turns $(a,b,c)$ into $(x,y)$. Then $A$ produces $(u,v,w)$. Finally, $C$ gives $(p,q)$.

<Flow variant="composition" />

The product is $CAB$: the rightmost matrix acts first. The matrices are:

<Math tex="\colB{B}=\begin{pmatrix}1&1&1\\3&-2&4\end{pmatrix},\quad \colA{A}=\begin{pmatrix}3&2\\7&-5\\-1&11\end{pmatrix},\quad \colC{C}=\begin{pmatrix}1&1&1\\3&-2&4\end{pmatrix}." display />

$B$ and $C$ happen to have the same entries, but occupy different positions in the chain.

## Combine two steps

Substitute $x=a+b+c$ and $y=3a-2b+4c$ into $u=3x+2y$:

<Math tex="u=3(a+b+c)+2(3a-2b+4c)=9a-b+11c." display />

Calculate the other two outputs in the same way. Together:

<Math tex="\colA{A}\colB{B}=\begin{pmatrix}9&-1&11\\-8&17&-13\\32&-23&43\end{pmatrix}." display />

If we combine $A$ followed by $C$ instead, we obtain:

<Math tex="\colC{C}\colA{A}=\begin{pmatrix}9&8\\-9&60\end{pmatrix}." display />

## Parentheses change the grouping

For compatible sizes, associativity gives $(CA)B=C(AB)$. Both sides describe the same chain. Only the intermediate matrix calculated first changes.

<Math tex="\colC{C}\colA{A}\colB{B}=\begin{pmatrix}33&-7&41\\171&-129&231\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb R^{2\times3}" display />

Thus the full substitution is $p=33a-7b+41c$ and $q=171a-129b+231c$.

<Note title="Regrouping is not reordering">Associativity lets you move parentheses, not matrices. $C(AB)$ equals $(CA)B$, but this does not imply equality with $ACB$.</Note>

## Dimensions before calculation

In the third exercise, products are checked by size alone. The matrices are:

$$
\begin{aligned}
A &\in \mathbb{R}^{2\times 1} \\
B &\in \mathbb{R}^{1\times 1} \\
C &\in \mathbb{R}^{1\times 3} \\
D &\in \mathbb{R}^{2\times 2} \\
E &\in \mathbb{R}^{3\times 1}
\end{aligned}
$$

A green tick (✓) means the product is defined; a dash (—) means it is undefined.

The next part reuses the letters with new sizes:

$$
\begin{aligned}
A &\in \mathbb{R}^{3\times 2} \\
B &\in \mathbb{R}^{1\times 3} \\
C &\in \mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}
\end{aligned}
$$

Of the six orders using each matrix once, only $BAC$ is defined. Its chain is $\mathbb R^2\xrightarrow{C}\mathbb R^2\xrightarrow{A}\mathbb R^3\xrightarrow{B}\mathbb R$.

<Math tex="\colA{A}\colC{C}=\begin{pmatrix}3&1\\0&2\\9&7\end{pmatrix},\qquad \colB{B}\colA{A}\colC{C}=\begin{pmatrix}-1&2&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}3&1\\0&2\\9&7\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}6&10\end{pmatrix}." display />

<Exercise question="Does matching every dimension make a composition automatically invertible?">
No. Compatible dimensions only guarantee that the composition is defined. A real matrix has an inverse precisely when it is square and has full rank. A projection, for example, may lose information.
</Exercise>
