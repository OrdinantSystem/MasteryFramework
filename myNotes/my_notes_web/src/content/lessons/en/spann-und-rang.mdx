## Dependence means one direction is enough

For $v=(1,2)$ and $w=(2,4)$, we have $w=2v$. Both lie on the same line through the origin. Combining them creates no second direction.

Two nonzero vectors are linearly dependent when one is a scalar multiple of the other. A pair containing the zero vector is also dependent.

For any number of vectors, the general definition is that they are independent if

<Math tex="\lambda_1v_1+\cdots+\lambda_nv_n=0" display />

can only hold with $\lambda_1=\cdots=\lambda_n=0$. Any other solution makes them dependent.

## Span collects every combination

The **span** is the set of vectors you can produce by taking linear combinations:

<Math tex="\operatorname{span}(v_1,\ldots,v_n)=\left\{\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda_kv_k\;\middle|\;\lambda_k\in\mathbb R\right\}." display />

One nonzero direction spans a line. Two independent directions span a plane. Two independent vectors in $\mathbb R^3$ therefore do not span the entire three-dimensional space.

## Rank counts reachable directions

The possible outputs of $A$ are exactly the combinations of its columns. Its image is the column space. The dimension of that space is the **rank**.

<Math tex="\operatorname{im}(\colA{A})=\operatorname{span}(a_1,\ldots,a_n),\qquad\operatorname{rank}(\colA{A})=\dim\operatorname{im}(\colA{A})." display />

<Interactive kind="vectors" preset="rank-collapse" id="vector-lab-rank" />

In the source example, the columns are $(1,3)$ and $(2,6)$: the second is twice the first. Although the matrix has two rows and two columns, every output lies on one line. The codomain is $\mathbb R^2$; the image has dimension $1$.

The interactive uses the additional examples $u=(2,1)$ and $v=(-1,2)$ or $v=(4,2)$. Dragging the second column lets you move between a plane and a line. The parallelogram makes the change in determinant visible.

<Note title="Columns are not automatically a basis">Matrix columns are images of the standard basis vectors. Those images can become dependent: a map can collapse independent input directions.</Note>

<Exercise question="A $3 \times  2$ matrix has two independent columns. What is its rank? Can its image be all of $\mathbb{R} ^3$?">
Its rank is $2$. Its image is a plane through the origin in $\mathbb R^3$, not the entire space. Rank $3$ would require at least three independent columns.
</Exercise>
