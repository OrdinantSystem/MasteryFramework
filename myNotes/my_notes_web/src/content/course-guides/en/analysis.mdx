### Questions to answer without looking

- Why does the converse of a true implication need a separate proof?
- What is the negation of “for every $x$ there exists $y$”?
- Where does the proof of $\sqrt{2}$ use the reduced-fraction assumption?
- Why must identity laws be derived before using them to prove idempotence?
- Why does the NAND implementation of AND need two gates rather than three?

For uncertain answers, revisit the linked explanations. The core route reaches sequence limits; Boolean laws and circuits are optional applications. Differentiation is beyond the present material.

### A combined problem

For integers $n$, test “even $n^2$ implies $4\mid n$.”

<Exercise>

Parity gives only even $n$, not divisibility by four. Counterexample $n=2$ has even square $4$ but is not divisible by $4$. A relevant theorem may prove less than the requested conclusion.

</Exercise>


### A sequence proof to reconstruct

Try the task before opening its solution. Check the order of choices and the entire tail, then revisit the convergence and failure lessons if your argument missed either.

<Exercise question="For $a_1=8$ and $a_n=0$ at every $n\ge2$, prove convergence to zero. Explain why the initial term is harmless, why $N=n+1$ is not an allowed threshold choice, and whether failure at target one would mean no real limit.">

For arbitrary $\varepsilon>0$, choose $N=2$. Every natural $n\ge N$ has $|a_n-0|=0<\varepsilon$. The initial term is outside this tail. N must be fixed before the arbitrary test index, so $N=n+1$ reverses the required dependence. At target one, $\varepsilon=1/2$ and $n=\max(N,2)$ defeat every proposed threshold. Nevertheless zero is a limit by the first argument: one failed target does not rule out all targets.

</Exercise>
