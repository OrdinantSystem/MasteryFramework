## Learner, purpose, and scope

University learners may be new to study methods and may study subjects other than mathematics. They need to choose a useful next study action, try it without prematurely seeing an answer, check their work, and revisit it later. No particular mathematics curriculum is a prerequisite. Mathematical illustrations need enough local explanation for their teaching purpose.

The course already has substantial prose, worked reasoning, source caveats, practice, and complete answers in many places. Success here means repairing named entry and practice gaps while preserving those assets. It does not mean reducing words by a percentage, adding a graphic to every page, or proving a learning benefit. No learner trial is available. Immediate, delayed, assisted, and unassisted results stay distinct.

## Teaching hypothesis and counterargument

Hypothesis: a familiar entry and explicit mechanism comparison let a novice choose an action without first learning unrelated mathematics; complete reference proofs let them check the session example. These are reasons for an editorial change, not measured effects.

Strong counterargument: five revised lessons can add repetition and turn a compact archive into a checklist. Response: preserve the existing short explanations, add only the named bridges/tasks, keep central reasoning visible, and use disclosures for complete answers. Retain twelve lessons. Do not add a new component or replace the existing question lens with another interface.
