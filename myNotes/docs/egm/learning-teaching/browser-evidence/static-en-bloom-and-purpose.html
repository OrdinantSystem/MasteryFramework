Bloom’s Taxonomy

PrerequisitesLearning Methodology

The six categories

Bloom’s revised taxonomy gives you a vocabulary for what a question asks you to do: remember, understand, apply, analyze, evaluate, or create. Stating a theorem from memory and deciding whether it applies to a new problem require different work, even when they concern the same theorem.

Here is a familiar topic: retrieval means attempting an answer before checking it. The following questions illustrate different demands on that topic; they are examples, not a study timetable.

CategoryExample task
RememberState what retrieval means, with the notes closed.
UnderstandExplain why reading the answer first changes what an attempt can show.
ApplyAttempt a short explanation without notes, then check it.
AnalyzeSeparate the attempt, feedback, and timing in a study routine.
EvaluateChoose a task for an observed learning gap and justify the choice.
CreateWrite a new retrieval question and reference answer; check that both fit the goal.

The graphic below uses a mathematical illustration. You can use the questions above without solving its proofs. A sequence is an indexed list of numbers: a1,a2,…a_1,a_2,\ldotsa1​,a2​,…, with positive index nnn. It is bounded if every term lies between fixed lower and upper bounds; an upper bound alone limits it only from above. Increasing means later terms are no smaller. Convergence to a limit means that for every positive tolerance there is a threshold index NNN from which every term stays within that tolerance of the same fixed number. The lens compares tasks about these ideas; it does not teach all their proofs.

Remember

State the precise definition of convergence of a real sequence.

Look for: Exact quantifiers and assumptions, without notes.

Understand

Explain why the terms must eventually stay within every chosen tolerance of the limit.

Look for: An accurate explanation and an example.

Apply

Prove directly that the sequence 1/n converges to zero.

Look for: Choose N for an arbitrary positive tolerance and justify the bound.

Analyze

Compare boundedness, monotonicity, and convergence. Which implications fail?

Look for: Explicit relationships and valid counterexamples.

Example · sequence convergence

Evaluate

For an unfamiliar sequence, choose a direct proof or a convergence theorem. Explain your criteria.

Look for: A defensible choice with the required hypotheses checked.

Example · sequence convergence

Create

Construct a bounded real sequence that does not converge, and prove both properties.

Look for: A checked construction; it need not be new research.

These are task types, not a proven ranking of learning effectiveness. Real tasks often combine them.

Try switching between the prompts above. Notice what you would have to produce for each one: a precise statement, an explanation, a proof, a comparison, a justified choice, or a checked construction. These categories can overlap in a single task. They are not a compulsory sequence or a scientific ranking of how much you learn. Creating a valid example of your own is enough to count as construction here; no new research is required.

Problem-oriented study

“How could I prove a real sequence converges if I do not know its limit?” gives boundedness and monotonicity a purpose before you study their definitions in detail.

You might begin with a prediction or a rough first step. If the terms and definitions are unfamiliar, ask for an explanation and a worked example. The early problem helps you see what the knowledge is for. It does not require you to discover a convergence theorem without instruction, and it does not remove the need to recall its exact assumptions later.

CHECK YOUR UNDERSTANDING

An increasing real sequence must converge to a real number. Which condition is missing, and how can you show it matters?

Show solution +

The theorem also needs an upper bound. The sequence an=na_n = nan​=n increases without bound, so it cannot converge to a real number. Conversely, boundedness alone cannot guarantee convergence: (−1)n(-1)^n(−1)n keeps alternating between two values. These counterexamples show what can go wrong when a hypothesis is removed; they do not say every sequence missing that hypothesis must diverge.

A worked comparison

Take an=1−1/na_n = 1 - 1/nan​=1−1/n for positive integers nnn. To use the monotone convergence theorem, first check growth: an+1−an=1/[n(n+1)]>0a_{n+1} - a_n = 1/[n(n + 1)] > 0an+1​−an​=1/[n(n+1)]>0. Next check a bound: 0≤an<10 \le a_n < 10≤an​<1. The theorem now establishes that the sequence converges in R\mathbb{R}R. Determining its limit as 1 is a further step, which a direct limit argument can supply.

Checking those hypotheses is application. Comparing this sequence with nnn and (−1)n(-1)^n(−1)n is analysis of where the conditions matter. Choosing between a direct proof and the theorem is evaluation: for an explicit formula, calculating a limit may be straightforward; for a recursive sequence whose limit is unknown, proving existence first may help. Choose a question because it practises a needed skill, rather than because its Bloom label sounds more advanced.
