Subtracting five from twelve suggests seven. To finish, check the original request: seven is an integer and seven plus five equals twelve. Discovery finds a candidate; verification establishes that it works.

<Math tex="\exists n\in\mathbb Z\;(n+5=12)." display />

## A complete direct proof

Choose $n=7$. It belongs to $\mathbb Z$. It satisfies $7+5=12$. Therefore an allowed object meets the requirement and existence follows. These four moves are candidate, domain, full predicate and conclusion. A single test proves this existential claim because the claim asks only for one success. It says nothing about uniqueness.

“Constructive” here means explicitly producing and checking an object. We remain in classical mathematics, where existence proofs need not always name a witness. “Choose an integer that works; therefore one exists” is circular: it assumes a successful choice is available. Choosing a known integer, then proving its property, avoids that assumption.

## Backward work and domain checks

From $2x=1$, backward work forces $x=1/2$. This proves real existence after checking membership and substitution, but cannot supply an integer witness. Necessary-candidate reasoning alone is not existence. Fully reversible algebra can prove an equivalence and, with domain checks, be sufficient; backward reasoning is not inherently invalid. A false assertion has no valid proof merely because a task asks for one.

For $4<n<8$, choose five and check integer membership and both strict inequalities. Four fails at the boundary. Six or seven would work equally well. For an arbitrary real input $x$, the rule $x+1$ constructs a larger real: addition stays in the reals and adding positive one increases the input. Testing only at zero would not establish this general rule.

### Practice

Prove $\exists n\in\mathbb Z\;(n+8=3)$.

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>

Negative integers are allowed.

</details>

<Exercise>

Choose $-5\in\mathbb Z$. Since $-5+8=3$, it satisfies the original equation and proves existence.

</Exercise>

Complete “Take one half” for a real between zero and one.

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>

Check all three requirements.

</details>

<Exercise>

$1/2\in\mathbb R$ and $0<1/2<1$, so it is an allowed witness. This does not assert uniqueness.

</Exercise>

Repair “Choose an integer with square $25$; therefore it exists”.

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>

Name a candidate without assuming success.

</details>

<Exercise>

Choose five, an integer, and verify $5\cdot5=25$. The original assumed the conclusion by assuming the successful choice.

</Exercise>

Construct an integer satisfying $n>4$ and $n<8$. Is the answer unique?

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>

Avoid endpoints.

</details>

<Exercise>

Six is an integer with $4<6<8$. Five and seven also pass. Explicit construction does not require uniqueness.

</Exercise>

Why does $x+1$ work for every real input? Why is testing zero insufficient?

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>

Use closure and positivity.

</details>

<Exercise>

Real addition gives a real output, and positive one increases any input. A test at zero covers one input only.

</Exercise>

Later, write a new four-sentence proof and label the job of each sentence.
