For this lesson, call an integer **admissible** if and only if it is positive, less than ten, and even. This is a local teaching label, not a standard class of integers. Six passes every check. Nine fails evenness; ten fails the strict upper bound. Passing two checks does not compensate for failing the third.

We use ordinary integer arithmetic and order as background. An even integer is one divisible by two with an integer result; zero is even but not positive.

## Read one structured criterion

Start by fixing the domain and the object. Each condition concerns the **same integer**. A positive noninteger below ten is not an admissible integer. Checking positivity on nine and evenness on negative two does not establish a conjunction about one integer, much less about a particular hidden card.

Let $A$ mean positive, $B$ less than ten, and $C$ even. Both

$$(A\land B)\land C\qquad\text{and}\qquad A\land(B\land C)$$

require all three claims. In the first grouping, $A$ and $B$ must hold, and $C$ must hold too. In the second, $B$ and $C$ must hold, as must $A$. This proves associativity semantically for any three propositions. It does not depend on checking four integers. Pure inclusive “or” also permits either grouping: each requires at least one of three inputs to be true. Mixed connectives need separate grouping checks.

**EN:** $A\land(B\land C)$: “A holds, and both B and C hold.” **DE:** „A gilt, und B und C gelten beide“. **ES:** «se cumple a, y se cumplen be y ce». “Simultaneous” here means satisfaction in the specified context, not necessarily events occurring at one moment. Conjunction does not bind a free variable.

A useful reading method is: fix domain and object; separate the conditions; check whether the list means all or at least one; preserve inner alternatives; decide whether the sentence is a definition or only a claim. Commas alone do not determine its connective.

## Establish a label, then unpack it

“If and only if” gives a full criterion. To prove that six is admissible, check $6>0$, $6<10$, and that $6/2=3$ is an integer. Conversely, knowing an integer is admissible gives **each** of those three properties. It does not give a unique value.

The complete list is $2,4,6,8$. The bounds leave only the integers $1$ through $9$; their even members are exactly the four listed. That exhausts the candidates. Exhibiting four successes without excluding others would not prove completeness. Zero fails positivity; ten fails the strict bound. The definition fixes the meaning; the candidate argument justifies the list.

## A sufficient rule is different

Suppose an ordinary rule says: “An integer receives a blue mark if it is positive, below ten, and even.” Assume this is **not** a full definition of the marking scheme. Passing the checklist guarantees a mark, but the rule does not forbid extra marks.

Marking every integer blue obeys that rule, yet nine is marked and odd. Thus a mark alone does not imply evenness. Replacing the rule by “marked **if and only if** positive, below ten, and even” would authorize unpacking the checklist.

A definition sometimes uses the shorter word “if” while intending a full criterion; that intention comes from the defining context, not from a general equivalence of “if” and “iff.” Write “means” or “if and only if” when both directions are intended. Flag ambiguous text rather than silently adding the converse.

## Practice with alternatives and failures

An integer must be positive and must be even **or equal to three**, with that entire alternative grouped. Write the criterion and test $3,6,-2,9$.

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>

There are two outer requirements. The second can succeed in either of two ways.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Reference answer</summary>

$P\land(E\lor T)$, where $P$ is positive, $E$ even, and $T$ equal to three. Three passes through positivity and $T$; six through positivity and $E$. Negative two fails positivity; nine fails both inner alternatives. The alternative is one grouped checklist item, not a requirement to satisfy all three letters.

</details>

<Exercise question="An integer qualifies when it is zero, or when it is positive and below ten. With A, B, C naming those claims in order, write the criterion. Do zero and ten qualify?">

$A\lor(B\land C)$. Zero qualifies through $A$ without needing positivity. Ten fails $A$ and $C$, so it qualifies through neither route. Requiring positivity of zero would change the outer connective.

</Exercise>

<Exercise question="For the original admissible integers, unpack every fact from the label. Must the integer equal six? Describe nonadmissibility, and use zero, nine, and ten to check its boundaries.">

The integer is positive, below ten, and even; it need not be six, since two also qualifies. Nonadmissibility means $n\le0$ **or** $n\ge10$ **or** $n$ is not even, with $n\in\mathbb Z$ still required. Zero fails only positivity, nine only evenness, ten only the strict upper bound. None forces all checks to fail. [Negating combined conditions](../negating-combined-conditions/) proves the underlying laws.

</Exercise>

Invent a new three-condition definition and a near-miss failing only one check. Then replace its iff by an ordinary one-way if and identify which inference from the label is no longer justified.
