“It is not the case that the number is unequal to three” says that the number equals three. The inner claim denies equality; the outer phrase denies that whole denial. The number itself does not change.

## The classical theorem

For any proposition $P$,

$$\neg(\neg P)\Leftrightarrow P.$$

**EN:** “not (not P) if and only if P.” **DE:** „nicht (nicht P) genau dann, wenn P gilt“. **ES:** «no (no pe) si y solo si pe».

This is an equivalence of truth values in every case, not a claim that $P$ is true. It is derived from classical bivalence and the truth-reversing definition of negation. It is not a statement about every logical system.

## Proof: both possible starting values

Take an arbitrary proposition. If $P$ is true, $\neg P$ is false, so $\neg(\neg P)$ is true. The original and the double negation agree.

If $P$ is false, $\neg P$ is true, so $\neg(\neg P)$ is false. They agree again. In particular, two negations have not made a false proposition true.

There is no third starting truth value in this setting. These two cases exhaust all possibilities, proving the theorem without assuming it. A numerical check of one true equality would illustrate the first case and leave the second unproved.

## Scope determines whether a pair can be removed

Substituting a whole equality gives

$$\neg\bigl(\neg(x=0)\bigr)\Leftrightarrow x=0.$$

For $x=0$ both sides are true; for $x=2$ both are false. The theorem explains agreement for every allowed input. A free $x$ remains free, the domain remains fixed, and no operation takes the arithmetic negative of $x$. “Not unequal to zero” does not mean “negative.”

The proposition substituted for $P$ may itself be compound:

$$\neg\bigl(\neg(P\land Q)\bigr)\Leftrightarrow(P\land Q).$$

Treat the whole conjunction as one input. There is no need to move either negation inside it.

By contrast, $(\neg P)\land(\neg Q)$ has two negations of **different inputs**. Neither negates the result of the other. If $P=Q=1$, then $P\land Q$ is true, while $(\neg P)\land(\neg Q)$ is false. Deleting both signs changes the truth value.

With three nested negations, remove one nested pair to leave $\neg P$. With four, remove a pair and then the remaining pair to leave $P$. This uses the theorem repeatedly, not a rule about multiplying negative numbers.

## Practice with nested and separate denials

Assume $P$ is false. Reconstruct the two intermediate truth values. Then simplify three and four nested negations without using a sign mnemonic.

Each negation reverses the value of its entire input. Locate a pair where one denial takes the other denial as its input.

<details>
<summary>Reference answer</summary>

The first negation gives true; the second gives false, agreeing with $P$. In $\neg(\neg(\neg P))$, remove the outer nested pair to leave $\neg P$. In $\neg(\neg(\neg(\neg P)))$, remove the outer pair, obtaining $\neg(\neg P)$, then remove that pair to obtain $P$.

</details>

<Exercise question="Simplify $\neg(\neg(P\lor Q))$ without changing anything inside the disjunction. Why does the theorem apply? Can the two signs in $\neg P\land\neg Q$ also be deleted?">

The simplification is $P\lor Q$. The whole inclusive disjunction has one classical truth value, which is reversed twice. The separate negations cannot be deleted: for $P=Q=1$ the proposed deletion changes false to true. Counting two negative marks does not establish nested scope.

</Exercise>

## Negative words in ordinary speech

Repeated negative words do not always express two nested logical operators. Spanish «No vino nadie» has negative concord: it says that nobody came, not that somebody did. Other languages also have constructions whose meaning cannot be read by counting negatives.

“I have not proved that $P$ is false” concerns a proof, not a direct denial of $\neg P$. It does not establish $P$. Translate the intended scope first.
