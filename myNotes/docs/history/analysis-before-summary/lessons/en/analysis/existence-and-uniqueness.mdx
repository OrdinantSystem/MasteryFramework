## Existence and witnesses

<span id="from-existence-and-witnesses-candidate-membership-whole-condition"></span>

### Candidate, membership, whole condition

For $\exists n\in\mathbb Z\;(n+5=12)$, seven is a witness: it is an integer and $7+5=12$. The witness is the object; the verification is the argument about it. A candidate becomes a witness once both checks are justified. For $4<n<8$, five passes both strict bounds. Nine passes only the lower bound and three only the upper: they cannot be combined. The repeated variable means the same object throughout.

A club needs a member available Tuesday and trained in first aid. Ada being available and Ben being trained do not suffice. Cora qualifies if she belongs to the club and has both qualities. Someone outside the club fails membership even with both qualities.

One half solves $2x=1$ over the reals, not over the integers. No integer solves it: each integer is at most zero or at least one, so its double is at most zero or at least two. Similarly, $0<x<1$ has the real witness $1/2$ but no integer witness. These arguments exclude all integers. Failed samples alone exclude only those tried; a finite search disproves existence only when it exhausts the domain.

## Constructing a witness

Return to the checked witness seven. Subtracting five from twelve explains how to discover it; the direct proof below separates discovery from verification.

<Math tex="\exists n\in\mathbb Z\;(n+5=12)." display />


<span id="from-constructing-a-witness-a-complete-direct-proof"></span>

### A complete direct proof

Choose $n=7$. It belongs to $\mathbb Z$. It satisfies $7+5=12$. Therefore an allowed object meets the requirement and existence follows. These four moves are candidate, domain, full predicate and conclusion. A single test proves this existential claim because the claim asks only for one success. It says nothing about uniqueness.

“Constructive” here means explicitly producing and checking an object. We remain in classical mathematics, where existence proofs need not always name a witness. “Choose an integer that works; therefore one exists” is circular: it assumes a successful choice is available. Choosing a known integer, then proving its property, avoids that assumption.


<span id="from-constructing-a-witness-backward-work-and-domain-checks"></span>

### Backward work and domain checks

From $2x=1$, backward work forces $x=1/2$. This proves real existence after checking membership and substitution, but cannot supply an integer witness. Necessary-candidate reasoning alone is not existence. Fully reversible algebra can prove an equivalence and, with domain checks, be sufficient; backward reasoning is not inherently invalid. A false assertion has no valid proof merely because a task asks for one.

In the interval example $4<n<8$, the checked witness five can be replaced by six or seven; four fails at the strict boundary. For an arbitrary real input $x$, the rule $x+1$ constructs a larger real: addition stays in the reals and adding positive one increases the input. Testing only at zero would not establish this general rule.

<span id="from-existence-and-uniqueness"></span>

## Existence and uniqueness

Two different keys open a lock. Checking the first proves that an opening key exists; checking the second does not invalidate that proof. It refutes a different claim: exactly one key opens the lock. Fix the drawer of allowed keys before counting.


<span id="from-existence-and-uniqueness-three-different-requirements"></span>

### Three different requirements

Existence means at least one allowed success. At most one means that no two distinct allowed objects succeed; zero is permitted. Unique existence means exactly one: existence together with at most one. In an empty drawer, existence is false, at most one is true and unique existence is false. Ruling out a second success does not supply a first.

$$
\exists! x\in D\;P(x).
$$

The exclamation mark adds the at-most-one obligation. It is not emphasis. First provide a witness in $D$ satisfying $P$. Then show that any object in $D$ satisfying $P$ equals that witness. Objects failing $P$ need not equal it. Equality concerns objects or values, not how expressions are spelled: $1/2$ and $2/4$ name the same real number.


<span id="from-existence-and-uniqueness-a-proof-with-both-obligations"></span>

### A proof with both obligations

$$
\exists! n\in\mathbb Z\;(n+5=12).
$$

The direct proof above supplies existence. For uniqueness, let $n$ be any integer satisfying $n+5=12$. Subtracting five from both sides gives $n=7$. Thus every possible solution equals the checked witness. We used an arbitrary solution, not an arbitrary integer assumed to be seven, and not a finite sample of failed alternatives.

Now restrict the domain to integers greater than ten. The same subtraction forces any hypothetical solution to be seven, outside the domain. At most one still holds, but existence fails. By contrast, for $4<n<8$, five and six are distinct integer witnesses, so uniqueness fails without needing a complete list.

## Counting witnesses

Cards labelled seven and nine both witness odd-card existence; choosing seven does not remove nine. For the interval example, we have already checked existence and refuted uniqueness; finding all witnesses adds an exclusion obligation.

One checked object proves existence. Two distinct checked objects refute at most one, without asserting exactly two. A complete list needs both verification of each entry and an argument excluding every unlisted object. Domain size is not witness count: an infinite domain can have zero, one, finitely many or infinitely many successes.

$$
\{n\in\mathbb Z:4<n<8\}=\{5,6,7\}.
$$

Each listed number is an integer and passes both bounds. Conversely, an integer above four is at least five and one below eight is at most seven. Thus any witness is five, six or seven. This exclusion argument, using the spacing of integers, is what makes the list complete. Trying three successful numbers alone would not.


<span id="from-counting-witnesses-changing-the-domain-changes-the-count"></span>

### Changing the domain changes the count

Over the reals, $5.5$ and $6.25$ also work. There are infinitely many witnesses. Start with five and six; insert their midpoint. Then repeatedly insert the midpoint between five and the last inserted value. Each result is real, above five and below the preceding result, hence still between four and eight. It is different from five and from every earlier inserted value. However many steps we take, another new witness can be added. This uses ordinary midpoint arithmetic and order, not a convergence theorem.

Distinct expressions need not be distinct objects: $1/2=2/4$. But two physical cards carrying the same label seven are distinct cards, although there is only one distinct numerical label. State what you count. An empty tray has no candidates; an even-only tray has candidates but no odd successes. Both make odd-card existence false. A universal statement on an empty tray remains true without supplying any witness.
